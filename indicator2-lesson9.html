🔍 Lesson 9 — Evaluating Data Quality and Reliability

⏱️ 100 minutes 📘 Indicator 2 🗓️ Semester 1 · Final 🎓 Grade 9

🧭 Lesson Opener

"Two websites say the same thing. One has 10 respondents. The other has 10,000. Which would you trust? The answer seems obvious — but only if you check."

Not all data is created equal. Some data is accurate, complete, and reliable. Some is biased, outdated, or misleading. Before you make a decision based on data, you must learn to evaluate its quality. In this lesson, you will learn the five criteria for evaluating data quality and how to spot bias.

🎯 What You Will Learn

  • Evaluate data quality using five criteria: accuracy, completeness, consistency, timeliness, relevance.
  • Identify common sources of bias in data.
  • Decide whether data is reliable enough for a decision.
  • Perform a data quality audit on a real dataset.

📖 1. The Five Criteria of Data Quality

When you look at a data set — whether from your own survey or from an online source — ask five questions about it.

1) Accuracy

Is the data correct? Could it have been mismeasured or misreported? For example, if a survey asks "How many hours do you study?" but students guess, the data might be inaccurate.

2) Completeness

Are all values present? If 30% of your survey responses skipped the key question, your data is incomplete and might not represent the whole group.

3) Consistency

Is the data consistent across sources? If three weather websites report three different temperatures for the same city on the same day, something is wrong.

4) Timeliness

Is the data recent? Data from 2015 might not reflect 2024 conditions, especially in fast-changing areas like technology or prices.

5) Relevance

Does the data answer your question? Global statistics might not apply to your school or community.

Key idea: Always check the source, sample size, and collection method before trusting any data.

Activity 1 — Which Criterion Is Broken? 8 min

Each scenario has a data quality problem. Write which of the five criteria is broken.

  1. A survey of 100 students, but only 40 answered the most important question.
  2. A weather report from 1995 used to plan a 2024 trip.
  3. Two government websites report different population numbers for the same year.
  4. A study on breakfast habits in France used to decide what Thai students should eat.
  5. A survey where respondents guessed their own height.

Compare with a partner. Which one was hardest to classify?

📖 2. Sources of Bias

Bias means that the data is skewed in one direction — it does not represent the whole truth. Here are the four most common types.

1) Sampling Bias

When the sample does not represent the population. Example: surveying only your friends and claiming the results apply to the whole school.

2) Response Bias

When people give inaccurate answers. Example: people might underreport how much junk food they eat because they feel embarrassed.

3) Question Bias

When the wording of a question pushes the answer. Example: "How much do you love our amazing new cafeteria?" — the question assumes the answer.

4) Selection Bias

When certain people are more likely to be included than others. Example: an online survey about technology use will naturally overrepresent people who use technology more.

Tip: When you spot bias, do not throw the data away. Instead, ask: "How much does this bias affect the conclusion?" Sometimes small biases are acceptable; sometimes they make the data useless.

Activity 2 — Spot the Bias 10 min

For each scenario, name the type of bias and suggest one way to fix it.

  1. A school surveys only Grade 12 students to decide what snacks to sell to all grades.
  2. A health survey asks: "How many hours per week do you exercise? (Most people do 3+.)"
  3. A poll about phone usage is conducted on a social media app.
  4. A survey about homework asks students to estimate their own study hours.

Share with the class. Which bias is the hardest to avoid?

📖 3. Is the Data Reliable Enough?

After checking the five criteria and identifying bias, you must decide: is the data reliable enough for your decision?

Use this checklist:

  • Source: Is the source credible (government, university, expert)?
  • Sample size: Is the sample large enough (usually 30+)?
  • Method: Was the data collected fairly (random sampling, neutral questions)?
  • Date: Is the data recent enough for your question?
  • Bias: Is there any obvious bias that could distort the results?

If most answers are yes, the data is probably reliable. If several answers are no, find another source.

Example: A study on sleep habits from a university with 5,000 participants, collected last year, using a random sample — this is highly reliable. A Facebook poll from 2018 with 20 respondents — not reliable.

Activity 3 — Evaluate the Source 10 min

Choose one of the scenarios below. Evaluate the data's reliability using the checklist. Write one paragraph (4–6 sentences).

  1. A news article citing "a study of 50 people" that says coffee cures headaches.
  2. A government report with 10 years of weather data from 200 stations across Thailand.
  3. A website saying "9 out of 10 doctors recommend our product," but no source is given.

Share your evaluation with a partner.

🔍 Case Study — How a Class Audited Its Own Data

A Grade 9 class collected a survey about phone usage. Then they audited their own data using the five criteria.

Accuracy: Students estimated their own usage. Some reported round numbers like "5 hours" when their phone logs might show 4.2. Rating: Medium.

Completeness: 100 responses, but 12 students skipped the "how many minutes do you call?" question. Rating: Good, with a note.

Consistency: Two questions asked about the same topic in different ways, and the answers matched. Rating: Good.

Timeliness: Data collected this semester. Rating: Excellent.

Relevance: Directly answers "how much do Grade 9 students use their phones?" Rating: Excellent.

Bias identified: All respondents were from one classroom (convenience sample). This is a form of sampling bias.

Conclusion: The data is fairly reliable for describing this classroom, but should not be generalized to the whole school without a larger sample.

Reflect: What would you change if you redid the survey?

🛠️ Hands-On Task — Data Quality Audit

Working in your group, perform a full data quality audit on your own survey data.

  1. For each of the five criteria, write a rating (Excellent, Good, Medium, Poor) and one sentence explaining it.
  2. Identify at least one source of bias in your data.
  3. Write a final verdict: "Our data is reliable for ______ because ______."
  4. Suggest one improvement for next time.

Present your audit to another group. Compare their ratings to yours — did you find the same issues?

📝 Reflection & Review

  1. Name the five criteria for evaluating data quality.
  2. What is the difference between sampling bias and question bias?
  3. Why is it dangerous to trust data without checking the source?
  4. What did you discover about your own survey data during the audit?

🔑 Key Takeaways

  • Evaluate data quality using accuracy, completeness, consistency, timeliness, and relevance.
  • Bias comes in four common forms: sampling, response, question, and selection.
  • Always check the source, sample size, method, and date.
  • Even your own data can have bias — auditing it makes you a better researcher.

🏠 Homework

Find a data set online (from a news article, government site, or social media post). Perform a data quality audit using the five criteria. Write a paragraph (6–8 sentences) summarizing your findings and stating whether you would trust the data for a real decision.
