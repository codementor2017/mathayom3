✅ Lesson 7 — Verifying Before Sharing

⏱️ 100 minutes 📘 Indicator 4 🗓️ Semester 2 · Final 🎓 Grade 9

🧭 Lesson Opener

"Have you ever shared a post without checking if it was true? What happened next? Did you regret it? Sharing is publishing — and you are responsible for what you share."

In this lesson, you will learn a five-step verification process you can use before sharing any post, image, or claim. You will also learn the share test — five questions that help you decide whether sharing is a good idea.

Image 7.1 — Lesson Opener Description: A warm parchment banner showing a hand hovering over a "Share" button. A small pause symbol floats above the hand. Behind it, a checkmark appears. Warm bronze tones.

🎯 What You Will Learn

  • Apply the five-step verification before sharing.
  • Use the share test to decide whether to share.
  • Use fact-checking tools to confirm claims.
  • Commit to responsible sharing.

📖 1. The Five-Step Verification

Before you share anything, follow these five steps.

Step 1 — Pause

Do not share immediately. Give yourself time to think. Emotional content spreads faster than facts, so slow down when something feels urgent.

Step 2 — Check the Source

Who posted it? Is it a known reliable source, or an anonymous account? Look at the account's history. Does it regularly post true information?

Step 3 — Cross-Reference

Do other reliable sources report the same thing? If only one account is posting it, be suspicious.

Step 4 — Check the Date

Is it old news presented as new? Old photos and articles are often recycled to seem current.

Step 5 — Use Fact-Checking Tools

Snopes, FactCheck.org, Thai Fact Check, and AFP Fact Check publish verified information on common claims.

Key idea: Sharing is publishing. You are responsible for what you share — even if you did not create it.

Activity 1 — Apply the Five Steps 8 min

For each scenario, list the steps you would take before sharing.

  1. A shocking headline from an unknown website.
  2. A viral photo of a "newly discovered animal."
  3. A friend's post about a celebrity death.
  4. A political message in a group chat.

Compare with a partner. Which steps are the most important?

📖 2. The Share Test

Before hitting "Share," ask yourself these five questions.

  1. Is it true? Have I verified it?
  2. Is it helpful? Does it provide useful information?
  3. Is it necessary? Does the world need to know this?
  4. Is it kind? Would I want this shared about me?
  5. Would I want this shared about me? This is the golden rule of sharing.
Tip: If any answer is "no," do not share it. It is that simple.
Image 7.3 — The Share Test Description: A parchment poster with five questions in a vertical list. Each question has a small icon: a checkmark (true), a lightbulb (helpful), a bell (necessary), a heart (kind), a mirror (about me). Warm bronze tones.

Activity 2 — Apply the Share Test 10 min

For each scenario, use the share test to decide: share, do not share, or share with correction.

  1. A funny meme about a politician.
  2. A rumor about a local business.
  3. A verified news article about a natural disaster.
  4. A friend's embarrassing photo.
  5. A viral video that might be fake.

Share your reasoning with the class.

📖 3. Fact-Checking Tools

These tools make verification fast and reliable.

Snopes (snopes.com)

A well-known fact-checking site in English. Search any viral claim.

FactCheck.org

US-based site focusing on political claims.

Thai Fact Check (thaifact.org)

Thai-language fact-checking organization. Very useful for local claims.

AFP Fact Check

International fact-checking network with a Thai section.

Example: A viral claim says "drinking lemon juice cures cancer." Search it on Snopes or Thai Fact Check. You will likely find a detailed article explaining why the claim is false, and where it came from.

Activity 3 — Verify a Post 10 min

Choose one viral claim you have seen. Use the five-step verification and one fact-checking tool to verify it.

  1. What is the claim?
  2. Pause — what made you pause?
  3. Source — who posted it?
  4. Cross-Reference — did other sources report the same thing?
  5. Date — is it recent or old?
  6. Fact-Check — what did the tool say?
  7. Share test — would you share it now? Why or why not?

Present your verification to the class.

🔍 Case Study — The Unverified Share

A student named Ploy saw a Facebook post claiming a local school would close for a week due to an outbreak. She shared it with her class group chat without checking.

What happened:

  • The post was false — no outbreak had occurred.
  • Within an hour, hundreds of students were asking questions.
  • The school had to issue an official statement explaining there was no closure.
  • Ploy felt embarrassed but also learned a lesson.

What should have been done:

  • Pause before sharing.
  • Check the source — the post was from an anonymous page.
  • Check the school's official page — no announcement existed.
  • If she had verified, she would not have shared it.

Reflect: Ploy did not create the fake news — she only shared it. Why is sharing still a responsibility?

🛠️ Hands-On Task — Verification Challenge

Working in pairs, your teacher will give you three posts (or you can find your own). Verify each one using the five steps and one fact-checking tool.

  1. Pause.
  2. Check the source.
  3. Cross-reference.
  4. Check the date.
  5. Use fact-checking tools.
  6. Apply the share test.

For each post, write one of these decisions:

  • Share (verified and useful)
  • Do not share (unverified or harmful)
  • Share with correction (share the fact-check instead)

Present your decisions and reasoning to the class.

📝 Reflection & Review

  1. What are the five steps of verification before sharing?
  2. What is the share test, and why does it matter?
  3. Name two fact-checking tools.
  4. What will you do differently before sharing something from now on?

🔑 Key Takeaways

  • Five steps: Pause, Source, Cross-Reference, Date, Fact-Check.
  • The share test asks: true, helpful, necessary, kind, would I want this about me?
  • Sharing is publishing — you are responsible for what you share.
  • Use fact-checking tools: Snopes, FactCheck.org, Thai Fact Check, AFP Fact Check.
