🧭 Lesson 3 — The PROMPT Method for Verification

⏱️ 100 minutes 📘 Indicator 3 🗓️ Semester 2 · Midterm 🎓 Grade 9

🧭 Lesson Opener

"You see a viral image with a shocking claim. The caption says it's from a trusted news source, but the image looks strange. How do you find out the truth?"

Rather than checking a source's surface, the smartest approach is to trace the information back to its origin. In this lesson, you will learn the PROMPT method — a step-by-step tool for verifying any claim, image, or viral post. By the end, you will be able to trace information to its source in minutes.

📖 1. The PROMPT Method

PROMPT is a six-step checklist. Each letter stands for one question you should ask when verifying information.

P — Primary Source

Can you find the original source? Not the version that was shared, but the first place the information appeared.

R — Reasoning

Does the reasoning make sense? Does the claim follow logically from the evidence? Or is there a leap that doesn't add up?

O — Origin

Who created it? When? Where? Is the creator a known expert, a news outlet, or an anonymous account?

M — Motive

Why was it created? Who benefits from people believing it? Is someone trying to sell something, influence an opinion, or cause confusion?

P — Proof

What evidence is provided? Is there data, expert testimony, or documentation? Or just a strong claim?

T — Timeliness

Is the information recent? An old photo or study can be recycled and presented as new.

Key idea: PROMPT is not about deciding whether you "like" a claim. It is about tracing the claim back to a reliable source.

📖 2. Tools for Verification

These four tools help you apply PROMPT quickly and accurately.

1) Reverse Image Search

Upload an image to Google Images or TinEye. These tools find where else the image appears online. If it was posted years ago on an unrelated site, the current caption is probably misleading.

2) Quote Search

Search the exact quote in quotation marks. If it appears only on low-credibility sites, be cautious. If it appears in reliable news outlets or official statements, it is more likely true.

3) Citation Tracing

If an article cites a study, find the study. Does it actually say what the article claims? Many false stories rely on people not checking the original.

4) Archive Tools

The Wayback Machine (web.archive.org) shows old versions of websites. You can use it to see what a page said before it was edited — useful for catching retractions or edits.

Tip: Reverse image search is the single most powerful tool for verifying viral photos. Use it first whenever you see a shocking image.

📖 3. Applying PROMPT — A Worked Example

Claim: "A new study says chocolate cures cancer."

Let's apply PROMPT step by step.

P — Primary Source: Search for the study. Where is it published? Is it in a peer-reviewed journal? If no study can be found, the claim is likely false.

R — Reasoning: Does the claim follow from the evidence? A single study rarely proves something as big as "cures cancer." Look for context.

O — Origin: Who created the claim? A real news outlet? A chocolate company? A random blog?

M — Motive: Who benefits? If a chocolate company funded the study or the claim, the motive is suspicious.

P — Proof: What proof is offered? Sample size, peer review, replication? A single small study is weak evidence.

T — Timeliness: When was the study published? Old studies may not reflect current science.

Conclusion: Unless the study is peer-reviewed, large, and replicated, the claim is probably exaggerated. It is likely misinformation or clickbait.

🔍 Case Study — The Recycled Photo

A viral post showed a photo of a flooded city. The caption said: "Bangkok is under water — evacuate now!" The post was shared 50,000 times in a few hours.

Applying PROMPT:

P — Primary Source: The image was uploaded to a reverse image search. It first appeared in an article from 2011, during a different flood.

R — Reasoning: The reasoning is "flood photo = current flood." But the photo was old. The reasoning is invalid.

O — Origin: The post came from an anonymous account with few followers.

M — Motive: The account had previously posted sensational content for engagement.

P — Proof: No official news source confirmed the claim.

T — Timeliness: The photo was 13 years old.

Conclusion: Misinformation. The photo was real but taken years ago. The caption was misleading.

Reflect: How could reverse image search have stopped this post from spreading?

🛠️ Hands-On Task — PROMPT Investigation

Working in pairs, choose one viral post, image, or claim. Apply the PROMPT method fully.

  1. Find the primary source (or confirm no source exists).
  2. Evaluate the reasoning.
  3. Identify the origin (who, when, where).
  4. Determine the motive (who benefits).
  5. Check the proof (evidence provided).
  6. Check the timeliness (is it recent?).

Write a one-paragraph verdict: is the claim accurate, misleading, or false? Use evidence from each PROMPT step.

Present your verdict to another pair. Give each other feedback on the strength of your evidence.

🔑 Key Takeaways

  • The PROMPT method has six steps: Primary source, Reasoning, Origin, Motive, Proof, Timeliness.
  • Tools like reverse image search and citation tracing make verification fast.
  • Old photos or studies are often recycled with new captions to mislead.
  • Verification is a habit — with practice, it takes only a few minutes.
