📖 1. What Is an App?

The word app is short for application. An application is a piece of software built to help a person do something useful or enjoyable. Apps are everywhere — in your pocket, on your laptop, inside your television, and even inside your family's car.

Even though apps look very different from one another, every app shares three basic parts:

  • Purpose — what the app is built to do. A calculator's purpose is to do math quickly.
  • User — the person the app is made for. A school app is made for students and teachers.
  • Function — how the app does its job. A map app finds your location and draws a route.
Example: Think about the LINE app.
Purpose: send messages to friends.
User: anyone with a phone.
Function: type a message → tap send → message travels over the internet → friend receives it.

Almost every app you use was created to solve a real problem for a real person. Before we learn the four types of apps, let's pause and notice the apps already around you.

📖 2. The Four Types of Apps

Not every app runs the same way. Apps are grouped into four types based on where they run and how you get them. Learning these types helps you decide what kind of app you want to build in your final project.

1) Mobile Apps

Mobile apps run on phones and tablets. You install them from an app store (like Google Play or the Apple App Store). They use touch, camera, microphone, and location to interact with you. Examples: LINE, TikTok, Google Maps, Instagram.

2) Web Apps

Web apps run inside a web browser. You don't install them — you just open a link and log in. They work on any device with a browser. Examples: Google Docs, Gmail, YouTube, Google Sheets.

3) Desktop Apps

Desktop apps are installed directly on a computer. They usually do heavier work, like editing photos, writing long documents, or building 3D models. Examples: Microsoft Word, Photoshop, Premiere Pro, Minecraft (Java Edition).

4) Embedded Systems

Embedded systems are built into a device. You cannot install or delete them. They quietly do one job really well. Examples: the software inside a smart TV, a car's computer, a smartwatch, a washing machine, or a digital camera.

Tip: Ask yourself — "Did I install this from a store? Does it run in a browser? Is it on a computer? Or is it built into a device?" The answers will tell you the type.

📖 3. Apps Across Every Subject

Apps are not only for entertainment. They help us learn, work, and solve problems in every subject you study at school. Here are real examples:

Subject Example App Idea What It Helps You Do
Math Currency converter Convert THB to USD quickly while traveling
Science Cell division simulation Watch mitosis stages step by step
Language Thai tone mark program Identify the tone of a syllable
Social Studies Historical timeline app Scroll through events in chronological order
Health BMI tracker Calculate body mass index and track changes
Art Color mixer See what happens when colors blend

Notice something important: each app was created to solve a real problem for a specific user. The currency converter is for travelers. The cell division simulation is for science students. The BMI tracker is for people who want to stay healthy.

Big idea: In this unit, you will design and build your own app that integrates with one of these subjects. It could be a tool for Math, a simulation for Science, a quiz for Language — the choice is yours.

🔍 Case Study — The Calculator: A Small App That Changed Everything

Long before smartphones, people used their fingers, tally sticks, and eventually mechanical calculators to do math. In the 1970s, the first handheld electronic calculators appeared. They were expensive, heavy, and could only add, subtract, multiply, and divide.

Today, the calculator app is one of the simplest apps on your phone — but it still follows the same three-part rule you just learned:

  • Purpose: do quick, accurate arithmetic.
  • User: students, shoppers, engineers, anyone who needs a number fast.
  • Function: input two numbers → tap an operator → display the result.

The calculator app fits the "mobile app" type. It was installed from an app store, it runs on your phone, and it uses your touchscreen. Its interface is deliberately simple: number buttons, operator buttons, an equals button, and one output line. In the next lessons, you will learn how designers make interfaces like this one easy to use.

Reflect: What would happen if the calculator app had no "clear" button? What if the buttons were all the same size and color? Good design is not just about looking nice — it is about helping the user do their job.

🛠️ Hands-On Task — Analyze a Real App

Choose one app you use every day (LINE, Google Maps, TikTok, a calculator, a weather app, etc.). On a sheet of paper, create a small poster with these six parts:

  1. App name: ____________________
  2. Type: Mobile / Web / Desktop / Embedded (circle one)
  3. Purpose: What problem does it solve?
  4. User: Who is it mainly for?
  5. Input: What does the user type, tap, or say?
  6. Output: What does the app show, play, or send back?

Decorate your poster with small drawings or icons. When you finish, hang it on the classroom wall. We will do a short "gallery walk" in the last few minutes — students move around the room and read three posters, leaving one sticky note of feedback on each.
