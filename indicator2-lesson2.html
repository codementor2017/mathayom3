📝 Lesson 2 — Advanced Data Collection Methods

⏱️ 100 minutes 📘 Indicator 2 🗓️ Semester 1 · Final 🎓 Grade 9

🧭 Lesson Opener

"Have you ever answered a survey that asked 'Don't you agree our product is the best?' That question is almost impossible to say 'no' to — and it produces terrible data."

Good data does not happen by accident. It comes from well-designed questions, careful sampling, and ethical collection. In this lesson, you will learn how to design a survey, how to sample a group fairly, and how to collect data ethically. You will then create your own survey and collect real responses.

📖 1. Designing Good Survey Questions

A survey is only as good as its questions. Bad questions produce bad data — and bad data leads to bad decisions. Follow these five rules:

  1. Be clear. Ask one idea per question. "How often do you use social media and how many hours?" is confusing. Split it into two questions.
  2. Be neutral. Avoid leading questions. "How much do you love our product?" pushes the respondent. Instead: "Rate your satisfaction 1–5."
  3. Use scales for opinions. A 1–5 scale gives more detail than a simple yes/no.
  4. Include demographic questions. Age, grade level, or gender help you understand who answered.
  5. Avoid sensitive questions. Unless absolutely necessary, do not ask about income, religion, or personal issues.
Bad question: "Don't you think students waste too much time on phones?"
Good question: "On average, how many hours per day do you spend on your phone? (0–1, 1–3, 3–5, 5+)"

📖 2. Sampling — Who Should You Ask?

If you want to know how all Grade 9 students feel about something, you cannot ask everyone — that is too many people. Instead, you ask a sample — a smaller group that represents the whole. The way you choose your sample matters a lot.

Random Sample

Everyone in the group has an equal chance of being chosen. Example: put every Grade 9 student's name in a bag and draw 30 names. Random samples are the fairest.

Stratified Sample

Divide the population into groups (strata), then sample from each group. Example: if Grade 9 has 4 classrooms, choose 8 students from each classroom. This ensures every group is represented.

Convenience Sample

Ask the easiest people to reach — like your friends, or students in your classroom. Convenience samples are fast, but they are usually biased. If you only ask your friends, you only hear your friends' opinions.

Tip: If you want reliable results, use random or stratified sampling. Convenience sampling is fine for a quick check, but never for serious decisions.

📖 3. Collecting Data Ethically

When you collect data from people, you are responsible for treating them with respect. Ethical data collection follows four rules:

  1. Ask permission. Always tell people what the survey is for before they begin.
  2. Keep responses anonymous. Do not collect names unless absolutely necessary.
  3. Never force participation. People have the right to say "no."
  4. Store data securely. Do not share personal answers with anyone outside your team.
Why ethics matter: Trust is the foundation of good data. If people feel tricked or unsafe, they will not answer honestly — and your data will be worthless.

Activity 3 — Design Your Survey 10 min

Choose a topic (e.g., "Choosing a phone promotion for students"). Design 8 neutral, clear questions for your survey.

  • Include at least 2 demographic questions (grade level, gender, etc.).
  • Include at least 3 questions using a scale (1–5).
  • Include at least 1 open-ended question at the end.

Write your questions in your notebook. You will use them in the hands-on task.

🔍 Case Study — A Survey That Went Wrong

A Grade 9 team wanted to know which snack the school should sell at the next fundraiser. They asked 25 of their closest friends during lunch.

Result: 88% said "chocolate cookies." The team bought 300 cookies. On the day of the fundraiser, most students actually wanted something healthy — only 40 cookies were sold.

What went wrong?

  • The sample was a convenience sample — only their friends.
  • The sample was too small — 25 out of 300 students.
  • They did not include a demographic question to see if certain groups preferred different snacks.

How to fix it:

  • Use a random or stratified sample — 100 students across grade levels.
  • Ask a wider variety of questions, including open-ended ones.
  • Analyze responses by group (e.g., do Grade 7 students prefer something different from Grade 12?).

Reflect: What other small mistakes can ruin a survey?

🛠️ Hands-On Task — Create and Share Your Survey

Working in groups of 3–4, do the following:

  1. Finalize your questions. Use the 8 questions you designed in Activity 3. Review each one for neutrality and clarity.
  2. Create a Google Form. Add the questions, choose appropriate question types (multiple choice, scale, short answer), and set the form to accept anonymous responses.
  3. Share your form with at least 15 respondents. You can share by QR code, link, or class group chat.
  4. Explain the ethics: At the top of the form, write one short sentence telling respondents that responses are anonymous and voluntary.
  5. Track responses in the Google Form response sheet.

Record the number of responses you receive. You will analyze them in the next lesson.

🔑 Key Takeaways

  • Good survey questions are clear, neutral, and one idea per question.
  • Random and stratified sampling produce fairer data than convenience sampling.
  • Ethical collection means consent, anonymity, voluntary participation, and secure storage.
  • Small, biased samples lead to bad decisions.
