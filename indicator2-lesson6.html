🌾 Lesson 6 — Case Study: Agricultural Products for Local Soil

⏱️ 100 minutes 📘 Indicator 2 🗓️ Semester 1 · Final 🎓 Grade 9

🧭 Lesson Opener

"A farmer has one field. She can plant rice, cassava, sugarcane, or vegetables. Which one will grow best? Which one will earn the most? The answer depends on the soil — and on the market."

In this lesson, you will apply the same decision process you learned in Lesson 5 — but this time to agriculture. You will combine primary data (soil measurements) with secondary data (crop requirements and market prices) to recommend the best crop for a specific field. This is real-world science and economics working together.

📖 1. Understanding Soil

Different crops grow best in different soils. Before choosing what to plant, farmers need to know four key soil properties.

1) pH

pH measures how acidic or alkaline the soil is. The scale runs from 0 to 14:

  • Acidic — below 7 (common in many tropical soils).
  • Neutral — around 7.
  • Alkaline — above 7.

2) Nutrients

The three main nutrients are nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K). Different crops need different balances.

3) Texture

Soil can be sandy (drains fast), loamy (ideal), or clay (holds water). Most crops prefer loamy soil.

4) Drainage

Good drainage means water does not sit around the roots. Poor drainage can drown certain crops.

Key idea: Soil is not "good" or "bad" — it is suitable or unsuitable for a specific crop.

  2. Soil pH affects which crops can grow.
  3. Good drainage is only important for rice.

📖 2. Crop Requirements

Different crops have different needs. Here are four common Thai crops and their requirements:

Crop Ideal pH Water Needs Drainage
Rice 5.5–6.5 Very high Poor (flooded fields)
Cassava 5.5–6.5 Low Good (well-drained)
Sugarcane 6.0–7.5 High Good
Vegetables 6.0–7.0 Medium Good

Notice that rice actually prefers poor drainage — it grows in flooded fields. Cassava, on the other hand, would die in a flooded field. This is why we cannot simply plant the same crop everywhere.

Example decision: If a farmer has sandy, well-drained soil with pH 6.0, cassava is a strong candidate. Rice would fail (no flooding), but sugarcane might work if the farmer can irrigate.

📖 3. Combining Soil Data with Market Data

Growing a crop that suits the soil is only half the decision. The other half is the market — will someone buy it, and for how much?

Here is the process for choosing a crop:

  1. Primary data: Test the soil (pH, nutrients, drainage).
  2. Secondary data: Look up crop requirements and market prices.
  3. Criteria and weights: Soil suitability, water needs, market demand, cost.
  4. Evaluation matrix: Score each crop.
  5. Recommendation: Choose the crop with the highest weighted total.
Tip: A crop might be perfect for the soil but have no market. Another crop might have a great market but fail in the soil. The best choice balances both.

Activity 3 — Build the Matrix 10 min

Suppose a farm has soil pH 6.0, good drainage, and medium water. Consider four crops: rice, cassava, sugarcane, vegetables.

Set up an evaluation matrix with these criteria:

  • Soil suitability (weight 5)
  • Water needs (weight 3)
  • Market demand (weight 4)
  • Cost to grow (weight 2)

Score each crop 1–5 for each criterion, then calculate the totals. Which crop wins?

A farmer named Uncle Somchai has a 2-hectare field in Nakhon Ratchasima. He asked a group of Grade 9 students to help him choose a crop. Here is what they found.

Primary data (soil test):

🛠️ Hands-On Task — Create a Crop Recommendation

Working in your group, create a full recommendation for a farmer using a sample soil profile (or your own local soil if you have access to a test kit).

  1. Soil properties: pH, nutrients, drainage (either real or sample data).
  2. Candidate crops: List 3–5 crops that could be grown.
  3. Criteria and weights: Soil suitability, water, market demand, cost.
  4. Evaluation matrix: Score each crop.
  5. Recommendation: Write a paragraph recommending the best crop, with evidence.
  6. Charts: Include at least 2 charts (bar chart of crop scores, table of soil properties).

Present your recommendation to another group. Give each other feedback using the phrases: "One strength is..." and "One thing to improve is..."

📝 Reflection & Review

  2. Why can't a farmer just plant the crop with the highest market price?
  3. How did the evaluation matrix help make a fair decision?
  4. What other factors might a farmer consider that we did not include?

🔑 Key Takeaways

  • Soil properties (pH, nutrients, texture, drainage) determine what can grow.
  • Crop requirements vary widely — rice floods, cassava drains.
  • Good decisions combine soil data with market data.
  • The evaluation matrix works for agriculture just as well as for phone promotions.
