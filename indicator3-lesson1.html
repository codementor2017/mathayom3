🧠 Lesson 1 — Introduction to Information Literacy

⏱️ 100 minutes 📘 Indicator 3 🗓️ Semester 2 · Midterm 🎓 Grade 9

🧭 Lesson Opener

"Before you even arrived at school today, you were exposed to thousands of pieces of information — news headlines, social media posts, group chat messages, ads, videos, memes. How many of them were true? How could you tell?"

In the digital age, anyone can publish anything. Some of it is accurate. Some is misleading. Some is deliberately false. Information literacy is the skill of telling the difference. In this lesson, you will learn what information literacy means, why it matters, and how to recognize the different types of information you encounter every day.

  • Define information literacy in your own words.

  • Explain why information literacy matters in the digital age.
  • Identify the types of information you encounter daily.

📖 1. What Is Information Literacy?

Information literacy is the ability to find, evaluate, and use information effectively. It has three parts:

  • Find — knowing where to look for reliable information.
  • Evaluate — judging whether the information is accurate, trustworthy, and relevant.
  • Use — applying the information responsibly and ethically.

In the past, information came from encyclopedias, newspapers, and libraries. Those sources had editors and fact-checkers. Today, information comes from everywhere — social media, blogs, video platforms, group chats. There is no editor. That means you are the fact-checker.

Key idea: Information literacy is not about memorizing facts. It is about knowing how to tell a good source from a bad one — and how to use good information wisely.

Activity 1 — Find, Evaluate, Use 8 min

For each action below, write whether it belongs to Find, Evaluate, or Use.

  1. Searching Google for a fact about a historical event.
  2. Comparing two websites to see if they agree.
  3. Sharing an article with a friend after checking its source.
  4. Deciding that a social media post is unreliable.
  5. Asking an expert for a recommendation.

Share with a partner. Did you classify every action the same way?

📖 2. Six Types of Information

Not all false information is the same. Understanding the differences helps you respond correctly.

1) Accurate Information

Information that is correct and has been verified. Example: "Thailand's population is about 70 million."

2) Misinformation

False information that is shared without intent to harm. The person sharing it usually believes it is true. Example: "Drinking hot water kills viruses."

3) Disinformation

False information shared with intent to deceive or harm. Example: a fake political video made to influence voters.

4) Fake News

False information presented as news, often for clicks or money. Example: "Celebrity caught doing something shocking!" — but the story is entirely made up.

5) Propaganda

Biased information used to promote a cause, political view, or product. It may mix truth and falsehood.

6) Opinion

A personal belief or judgment. Opinion is not the same as misinformation — it is simply one person's view. Example: "Thai food is the best in the world."

Why the difference matters: Misinformation is usually spread by accident. Disinformation is spread on purpose. Both can cause harm, but the response is different. With misinformation, we correct gently. With disinformation, we may need to report the source.

Activity 2 — Classify the Information 10 min

Read each example. Write which type it belongs to.

  1. "A news article from a major newspaper reporting election results."
  2. "A viral social media post with no source claiming a cure for a disease."
  3. "A scientific study from a university, published in a peer-reviewed journal."
  4. "A meme mocking a politician."
  5. "A government report on rainfall."
  6. "A forwarded message in a family group chat saying a famous person died (but they are alive)."
  7. "An opinion column saying a certain movie is the best of the year."
  8. "A satirical news site that creates humorous fake headlines."
  9. "A verified fact-check confirming a claim is true."
  10. "An anonymous blog post claiming a secret government plan."

Discuss with a partner. Which items were hardest to classify? Why?

📖 3. Why Information Literacy Matters

Information literacy protects you in four important ways.

1) It Protects Your Health

False health claims can lead people to refuse vaccines, take harmful remedies, or ignore real medical advice. Good information saves lives.

2) It Protects Your Money

Scams and fake investment schemes rely on people who do not check their sources. Information literacy helps you spot the warning signs.

3) It Protects Your Choices

From voting to choosing a school, good decisions depend on good information. Misinformation can lead to bad decisions that affect years of your life.

4) It Protects Society

When misinformation spreads, trust in institutions falls, communities divide, and panic can result. Information literacy is a civic responsibility.

Tip: You do not need to be an expert in everything. You only need to know how to check a source, cross-reference, and think critically. These skills work in every subject.

Activity 3 — Information Audit 10 min

Think about your own social media feed or group chats from the last 24 hours. In your notebook, list:

  1. Three pieces of information you believe are reliable. Why?
  2. Three pieces you are unsure about. What would you check?
  3. One piece you suspect is misinformation. What makes you suspicious?

Share one example with a partner. Explain your reasoning.

🔍 Case Study — The Rumor That Emptied a Market

In 2020, a rumor spread through a Thai province: "A local market has been closed because of a dangerous outbreak." The message was shared over 10,000 times in three hours on a messaging app. By noon, the market was nearly empty, and vendors lost a full day of income.

What actually happened? The market was open. There had been no outbreak. The rumor had started as a misunderstanding and grew as people forwarded it without checking.

What type of information was it? Misinformation — false, but shared without harmful intent.

What was the impact?

  • Vendors lost money.
  • Customers were panicked.
  • Trust in the local market was damaged.

How could it have been prevented? One person could have paused, checked the market's official page, and shared the correction instead of forwarding the rumor.

Reflect: Have you ever shared something you later discovered was false? What would you do differently now?

🛠️ Hands-On Task — Analyze Three Posts

Your teacher will show three short posts (or headlines). In your notebook, analyze each one using the questions below.

  1. What type of information is this? (Accurate / Misinformation / Disinformation / Fake News / Propaganda / Opinion)
  2. Who created it? (Author, page, organization.)
  3. What is the purpose? (Inform / persuade / entertain / sell / mislead.)
  4. What evidence is provided?
  5. Would you share it? Why or why not?

When finished, share your analysis with a partner. Compare your classifications and discuss any differences.

🔑 Key Takeaways

  • Information literacy means finding, evaluating, and using information well.
  • Types of information include accurate, misinformation, disinformation, fake news, propaganda, and opinion.
  • Misinformation is spread without harmful intent; disinformation is deliberate.
  • Information literacy protects your health, money, choices, and society.
