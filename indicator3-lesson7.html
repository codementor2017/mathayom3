🎬 Lesson 7 — Analyzing Media

⏱️ 100 minutes 📘 Indicator 3 🗓️ Semester 2 · Midterm 🎓 Grade 9

🧭 Lesson Opener

"Every ad, every news story, every social media post is trying to do something — inform you, persuade you, sell you something, or entertain you. The first step to understanding media is asking: what is this trying to do?"

In this lesson, you will learn a media analysis framework — a set of six questions that help you understand any media message. You will then apply this framework to real ads, news articles, and social media posts.

🎯 What You Will Learn

  • Analyze the purpose of a media message.
  • Identify the target audience.
  • Detect bias and persuasion techniques.
  • Understand how framing shapes the message.

📖 1. The Six Questions of Media Analysis

Every media message can be analyzed with six simple questions.

1) Purpose

What is this trying to do? Four main purposes: inform, persuade, entertain, or sell. Some messages combine two or more.

2) Audience

Who is the target? Age, gender, interests, income, and location all shape how a message is designed.

3) Source

Who created it? A news agency, a company, a political party, a private person?

4) Techniques

What persuasion techniques are used? Common ones: emotional stories, expert testimonials, bandwagon (everyone is doing it), repetition, and fear.

5) Bias

Is there a bias? Who benefits if you believe the message? What is left out?

6) Framing

How is the story presented? What is emphasized? What is minimized or ignored? Two sources can tell the same story with very different framing.

Key idea: Media is never neutral — even the choice of which story to cover is a decision. Analyzing media is about noticing those decisions.

Activity 1 — Identify the Purpose 8 min

For each media item, write the main purpose: Inform, Persuade, Entertain, or Sell.

  1. A weather forecast on a news app.
  2. A political campaign video.
  3. A funny TikTok video.
  4. A commercial for a new phone.
  5. A documentary about climate change.
  6. A clickbait article titled "You won't believe what happened next!"

Compare with a partner. Which item was hardest to classify?

📖 2. Understanding Persuasion Techniques

Advertisers and politicians use specific techniques to influence your thinking. Here are the most common.

Emotional Appeal

Using feelings like joy, fear, or sadness to influence you. Example: an ad showing a happy family using a product.

Expert Testimonial

Using an expert or celebrity to endorse something. Example: "9 out of 10 dentists recommend..."

Bandwagon

"Everyone is doing it, so you should too." Example: "Over 10 million users!"

Repetition

Repeating a message until it feels true. Example: the same slogan in every ad.

Fear

Threatening a negative outcome if you don't act. Example: "Without insurance, your family could lose everything."

Framing

Presenting the same fact in different ways. Example: "90% success rate" vs. "10% failure rate." Same data, different impression.

Tip: Once you know these techniques, you will start seeing them everywhere. That awareness is what media literacy is all about.

Activity 2 — Spot the Technique 10 min

For each message, name the persuasion technique used.

  1. "Join millions of satisfied customers today!"
  2. "A doctor recommends this vitamin."
  3. A sad image of a child in a charity ad.
  4. "Act now or you'll regret it forever."
  5. "Our product has a 95% satisfaction rate."
  6. The same slogan plays in every ad for the same product.

Share with a partner. Which technique do you see most often in advertising?

📖 3. Framing — The Same Story, Told Two Ways

Framing is how the media chooses to present a story. The same facts can be framed in two very different ways — and each framing leads the reader to a different conclusion.

Example: A new tax law is passed.
Framing 1: "New law raises taxes on working families."
Framing 2: "New law funds hospitals and schools for the next decade."
Both use facts — but they leave very different impressions.

When analyzing framing, ask:

  • What is the headline emphasizing?
  • What is left out?
  • Which images, quotes, or examples are chosen?
  • Whose voices are heard, and whose are missing?
Image 7.4 — Framing Description: A parchment showing two news pages side by side. The left uses a red headline, angry faces, and a negative tone. The right uses a blue headline, calm faces, and a positive tone. Both show the same story. Warm bronze tones.

Activity 3 — Compare Two Framings 10 min

Your teacher will show two headlines covering the same event. In your notebook, answer:

  1. What is each headline emphasizing?
  2. What is left out of each?
  3. Which framing is more neutral? Why?
  4. How might each framing affect a reader's opinion?

🔍 Case Study — Analyzing an Advertisement

Below is a description of a phone ad. Analyze it using the six questions.

A 30-second TV commercial shows a young adult using a phone while hiking, laughing with friends, then relaxing at a café. Music is upbeat. A voiceover says: "The new Z-Phone. Live your best life." At the end, a short line of text says "From 25,000 THB."

Analysis:

  • Purpose: To sell a phone (and possibly to create a lifestyle image).
  • Audience: Young adults, especially those who value friendships and adventure.
  • Source: The phone company.
  • Techniques: Emotional appeal (happiness, friendship), testimonial (implied by the lifestyle), catchy music, and a compelling slogan.
  • Bias: The ad only shows positive uses. It doesn't mention costs, privacy issues, or the phone's limitations.
  • Framing: The phone is framed as a tool for living a meaningful life — not as a piece of technology.

Reflect: After watching this ad, do you want the phone? If yes, why? What did the ad actually show you about the product?

🛠️ Hands-On Task — Media Analysis

Working in groups, choose one media message (an ad, a news article, or a social media post). Analyze it using the six questions.

  1. Purpose — What is it trying to do?
  2. Audience — Who is the target?
  3. Source — Who created it?
  4. Techniques — What persuasion techniques are used?
  5. Bias — Is there bias? Who benefits?
  6. Framing — How is the story presented?

Present your analysis to the class. Each group has 4 minutes. Be ready to answer questions about why you classified it the way you did.

📝 Reflection & Review

  1. What are the six questions of media analysis?
  2. Name three persuasion techniques and give an example of each.
  3. What is framing? Why does it matter?
  4. What is one thing you will notice now that you didn't before?

🔑 Key Takeaways

  • Analyze media using six questions: Purpose, Audience, Source, Techniques, Bias, Framing.
  • Common persuasion techniques: emotional appeal, testimonial, bandwagon, repetition, fear, framing.
  • Framing shapes how the same facts are understood.
  • Media is never fully neutral — noticing that is a critical skill.
