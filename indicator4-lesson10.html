🏆 Lesson 10 — Final Project: Digital Citizenship Campaign

⏱️ 100 minutes 📘 Indicator 4 🗓️ Semester 2 · Final 🎓 Grade 9

🧭 Lesson Opener

"You have learned how to be safe, responsible, and legal online. Now it's time to teach others. Today, you will create a campaign that helps your school community become better digital citizens."

In this final lesson, you will create a Digital Citizenship Campaign that teaches others about safe, responsible, and legal IT use. You will design a poster series, video, social media campaign, or presentation, and share it with your school community.

Image 10.1 — Lesson Opener Description: A warm parchment banner showing a group of students holding posters with digital citizenship messages: "Protect Your Privacy," "Verify Before Sharing," "Respect Copyright." A small megaphone icon sits above them. Warm bronze tones.

🎯 What You Will Learn

  • Create a digital citizenship campaign that teaches others.
  • Apply all concepts from the unit: online safety, software, laws, fair use, fake news, verification, social media, rights.
  • Use CC-licensed images with attribution.
  • Present your campaign with clarity and confidence.
  • Reflect on what you have learned and how you will change.

📖 1. Your Project Task

Your task is to create a Digital Citizenship Campaign for your school. Your campaign must teach others about safe, responsible, and legal IT use.

Your campaign must include at least 5 of these 8 topics:

  1. Safe online transactions.
  2. Buying software and subscriptions safely.
  3. In-game purchases and digital items.
  4. Thai computer laws.
  5. Fair use of copyright.
  6. Not creating fake news.
  7. Verifying before sharing.
  8. Responsible social media use and digital rights.

Your campaign must also include:

  • At least 2 CC-licensed images with attribution (if you use images).
  • Practical tips for each topic.
  • A section on "What to do if something goes wrong."
  • A 5-minute presentation to the class.
  • A short written reflection.
Tip: Choose a format you enjoy. A poster series, a short video, a social media campaign, a school assembly, a brochure, or a website — all work well if your message is clear.
Image 10.2 — Campaign Formats Description: A parchment grid with six panels showing different campaign formats: a poster series, a video, a social media campaign, a school assembly, a brochure, and a website. Each panel has a small icon and label. Warm bronze frames.

Activity 1 — Choose Your Topics and Format 10 min

In your group, decide:

  1. Which 5+ topics will you cover?
  2. Which format will you use?
  3. Who is your target audience? (Younger students, parents, teachers, your class.)
  4. What are your key messages? One sentence for each topic.
  5. What images will you use? (Include CC sources if using photos.)

Get your plan approved by your teacher before you start creating.

📖 2. Planning Your Campaign

Use this structure to organize your campaign.

Section 1 — Introduction

What is digital citizenship? Why does it matter?

Section 2 — Your 5 Topics

For each topic, write a short paragraph and add 2–3 practical tips. Include a visual (icon, small image, or chart) for each.

Section 3 — What to Do If Something Goes Wrong

Include a practical guide: how to report scams, where to get help, who to contact.

Section 4 — Call to Action

End with a memorable message encouraging your audience to become responsible digital citizens.

Image 10.3 — Campaign Plan Template Description: A parchment template with four labeled sections: Introduction, 5 Topics, What to Do If Something Goes Wrong, Call to Action. Blank lines under each. A megaphone icon at the top. Warm parchment tones.

Activity 2 — Write Your Content 15 min

In your notebook or on your device, write the content for your campaign:

  1. Introduction (3–4 sentences).
  2. For each of your 5 topics: a short paragraph + 2–3 tips.
  3. "What to Do If Something Goes Wrong" (4–5 tips).
  4. Call to Action (2–3 sentences).

Show your content to your teacher before moving to the design stage.

📖 3. Creating Your Campaign

Now it's time to design and create. Follow these guidelines.

  • Keep text short and clear. Use bullet points.
  • Use visuals. Images, icons, or charts make messages memorable.
  • Use color wisely. 3–5 colors is enough.
  • Cite your sources. If you use images from the internet, make sure they are CC-licensed or public domain and give credit.
  • Make it age-appropriate. Your target audience should understand it easily.
Tip: For CC images, search on sites like Unsplash, Pixabay, or Wikimedia Commons. Always check the license and add attribution.
Image 10.4 — Creating Your Campaign Description: A cartoon group of students designing a poster on a large parchment. Colored pens, image printouts, and sticky notes are scattered on the table. Warm bronze tones.

Activity 3 — Check Your Work 10 min

Before presenting, check your campaign:

  1. Does it cover at least 5 topics?
  2. Are the tips practical and clear?
  3. Did you include the "What to Do If Something Goes Wrong" section?
  4. Did you use at least 2 CC images with attribution (if using images)?
  5. Is your Call to Action memorable?
  6. Is the campaign visually clear and age-appropriate?

Fix anything missing before presenting.

🔍 Case Study — A Campaign That Changed a School

Last year, a Grade 9 group created a "Stop Fake News" campaign. They produced:

  • A series of 5 posters (one per topic: verifying sources, checking photos, not sharing rumors, fact-checking tools, and the share test).
  • A 60-second video shown during morning assembly.
  • A one-page "how to fact-check" guide distributed to every classroom.
  • A social media campaign with daily tips for two weeks.

Result: Within one month, the school reported a 40% drop in students sharing unverified news in class group chats. Teachers said students were more careful about the information they shared.

Reflect: What made this campaign effective? (Hint: it was specific, visual, and repeated.)

🛠️ Hands-On Task — Prepare Your Presentation

You have about 15 minutes to prepare a 5-minute presentation. Your presentation must include:

  1. Introduction — What is your campaign about?
  2. Target audience — Who is it for?
  3. Topics covered — Which 5+ topics did you include?
  4. Key messages — One sentence per topic.
  5. Visual elements — Show your posters, video, or design.
  6. CC attributions — Show where your images came from.
  7. What you learned — One sentence about your biggest takeaway.

Practice with a partner. Give each other feedback on clarity and time.

Image 10.6 — Presentation Setup Description: A cartoon student practicing a presentation in front of a partner. A screen shows a campaign poster with sections and CC attributions. Warm parchment tones.

📖 4. Presenting Your Campaign

Each group presents for 5 minutes. While you are not presenting, take notes:

  • What was the campaign about?
  • Was the message clear?
  • What was the strongest part?
  • What would you have done differently?

After all presentations, the class will vote on the most effective campaign. The winning campaign will be displayed in the school (with the group's permission).

Image 10.7 — Class Presentations Description: A cartoon classroom with groups presenting their campaigns. Posters and screens show digital citizenship messages. The audience listens and takes notes. Warm parchment tones.

📝 Presentation & Reflection

After all presentations, write a short reflection in your notebook:

  1. What is the most important thing you learned in this unit?
  2. How will you change your online behavior?
  3. What did you enjoy most about creating your campaign?
  4. What would you improve if you had one more week?
  5. How will you share these lessons with others?

🔑 Key Takeaways

  • Digital citizenship means using technology safely, responsibly, and legally.
  • A good campaign has clear messages, practical tips, and good visuals.
  • Sharing your knowledge with others is a form of digital leadership.
  • Every choice online is a chance to be a better digital citizen.

🏠 Homework

Share your campaign with a family member or a friend outside your class. Explain what you created and why.

Then write a final reflection (6–8 sentences) answering:

"How has this entire unit changed the way I use technology? What is one habit I will keep for life?"

Submit your campaign and reflection at the start of the next lesson.
