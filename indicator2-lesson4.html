⚖️ Lesson 4 — Creating and Evaluating Alternatives

⏱️ 100 minutes 📘 Indicator 2 🗓️ Semester 1 · Final 🎓 Grade 9

🧭 Lesson Opener

"How do you decide which movie to watch with friends? You might consider genre, reviews, length, and who's watching. Not all of those matter equally — but each one counts a little."

When you have to choose between multiple options, you need a fair way to compare them. In this lesson, you will learn to build an evaluation matrix — a tool that scores each option against weighted criteria. It turns messy decisions into clear, data-driven ones.

Above them float four floating weights labeled "Genre," "Reviews," "Length," and "Friends' opinions." A small balance scale balances the weights on the table. Warm bronze tones.

🎯 What You Will Learn

  • List the steps for creating an evaluation matrix.
  • Assign weights to criteria based on importance.
  • Score each alternative against each criterion.
  • Choose the best alternative with evidence.
  • Explain how weighting criteria affects the final decision.

📖 1. Why Evaluation Matrices?

Imagine three friends want to choose a movie together. They argue for twenty minutes, each pushing their favorite. No one agrees. An evaluation matrix solves this by giving every movie a fair score against the same criteria.

The matrix has five steps:

  1. List the alternatives (the options you are choosing between).
  2. List the criteria (the things that matter — price, quality, speed, etc.).
  3. Assign a weight to each criterion (1–5). More important criteria get higher weights.
  4. Score each alternative for each criterion (1–5).
  5. Multiply weight × score, then sum the results. The highest total wins.
Why it works: The matrix makes every decision transparent. You can see exactly why one option scored higher than another.

Activity 1 — Choose Your Criteria 8 min

Suppose you are choosing a new backpack for school. In your notebook, list:

  • 4 alternatives (different backpack brands or styles).
  • 4 criteria you would use to evaluate them (e.g., price, size, comfort, style).
  • Assign a weight (1–5) to each criterion based on how much it matters to you.

Share with a partner. Did your weights differ? Why?

📖 2. How to Build the Matrix

Let's build a matrix together for choosing a phone promotion.

Alternatives: Promo A, Promo B, Promo C.

Criteria: Price, Data, Calls, Contract.

Step 1: Assign weights (1–5) to each criterion. Let's say:

  • Price: 5 (most important for a student)
  • Data: 4 (very important)
  • Calls: 3 (somewhat important)
  • Contract: 2 (less important)

Step 2: Score each promotion (1–5) for each criterion.

Step 3: Multiply weight × score, then sum.

CriterionWeightPromo APromo BPromo C
Price53 (15)4 (20)2 (10)
Data45 (20)3 (12)4 (16)
Calls32 (6)4 (12)5 (15)
Contract25 (10)3 (6)4 (8)
Total515049

Decision: Promo A wins, but only by one point. The matrix shows the three promotions are very close.

Key insight: Small changes to the weights can change the winner. If "Contract" had a weight of 5 instead of 2, Promo A would lose its advantage.

Activity 2 — Build Your Own Matrix 10 min

Use the backpack example from Activity 1. Build a full evaluation matrix:

  1. List the four alternatives as columns.
  2. List the four criteria as rows with their weights.
  3. Score each backpack (1–5) for each criterion.
  4. Multiply and sum for each alternative.
  5. Circle the highest total.

Then answer: If you changed the weight of "price" to 5 and "style" to 1, would the winner change?

📖 3. Sensitivity — Does the Weight Matter?

A good analyst does not just pick the highest total. They also test: "How sensitive is my decision to the weights I chose?"

This process is called sensitivity analysis. You change the weights slightly and see if the winner still wins. If it does, your decision is robust. If not, you should be careful.

Tip: If two options score very close, do not force a decision based on a 1-point difference. Consider gathering more data or adding another criterion.
Image 4.4 — Sensitivity Analysis Description: A cartoon parchment showing two identical matrices side by side. In the second matrix, one weight has been changed and the winner is now different. A small "what if?" bubble floats above with a question mark. Warm bronze tones.

Activity 3 — Sensitivity Test 10 min

Take the matrix you built in Activity 2. Change one weight (either up or down by 2). Recalculate the totals.

  1. Which criterion did you change?
  2. Did the winner change? If so, how?
  3. What does this tell you about your decision?

Share your findings with a partner. Discuss: which decisions are "robust" and which are "fragile"?

🛠️ Hands-On Task — Evaluate Your Own Alternatives

Working in your group, use the survey data you processed in Lesson 3 to build an evaluation matrix for a real decision.

  1. Choose a decision related to your survey (e.g., "Which phone promotion should a student choose?").
  2. List 3 alternatives as columns.
  3. List 4–5 criteria as rows.
  4. Assign weights (1–5) based on what your survey data revealed was important to students.
  5. Score each alternative.
  6. Multiply, sum, and circle the winner.
  7. Do one sensitivity test: change one weight by 2 and see if the winner changes.
  8. Write a short paragraph recommending the best alternative, using your data and matrix as evidence.

Save your matrix and paragraph — you will use them in the next two lessons.

📝 Reflection & Review

  1. What are the five steps of building an evaluation matrix?
  2. Why is it important to assign weights rather than treat all criteria equally?
  3. What is sensitivity analysis, and why does it matter?
  4. How did your matrix change the way you think about the decision?

🔑 Key Takeaways

  • An evaluation matrix makes complex decisions clear and fair.
  • Weights reflect how important each criterion is.
  • Even small changes in weights can change the winner — always do a sensitivity test.
  • Decisions backed by a matrix are easier to explain and defend.
