🔍 Lesson 2 — Evaluating Source Reliability

⏱️ 100 minutes 📘 Indicator 3 🗓️ Semester 2 · Midterm 🎓 Grade 9

🧭 Lesson Opener

"If you wanted to know whether a new health remedy actually works, who would you trust? A doctor? A government health agency? A scientific study? Or a social media influencer?"

Not all sources are equal. Some have credentials and evidence. Others have opinions and ads. In this lesson, you will learn a simple five-criteria framework for evaluating any source — and you will practice using it on real examples.

A large question mark floats above them, asking "Who do you trust?" Warm bronze tones.

🎯 What You Will Learn

  • Apply the five criteria for source reliability.
  • Cross-reference information from multiple sources.
  • Identify the signs of an unreliable source.
  • Decide whether to trust a source in a real situation.

📖 1. The Five Criteria

When you find a source, ask these five questions.

1) Authority — Who wrote it?

Is the author named? Do they have credentials in the topic? Is the website a known organization (university, government, established news outlet) or an anonymous blog?

2) Accuracy — Is the information correct?

Can you verify the facts elsewhere? Are there citations or links to primary sources? Are statistics clearly sourced?

3) Objectivity — Is it biased?

Who benefits from this information? Is the source trying to sell something, promote a political view, or inform neutrally? Look for loaded language and one-sided arguments.

4) Currency — Is it up to date?

When was it published or last updated? For fast-changing topics (technology, medicine, prices), even a two-year-old article can be outdated.

5) Coverage — Does it cover the topic fully?

Does the source give you the full picture, or only one side? Does it explain context, or just present isolated facts?

Tip: You can remember the five criteria as A-A-O-C-C (Authority, Accuracy, Objectivity, Currency, Coverage).

Activity 1 — Match the Criterion 8 min

Read each question. Write which of the five criteria it relates to.

  1. "When was this article last updated?"
  2. "Does the writer have a degree in this field?"
  3. "Are all the facts consistent with other trusted sources?"
  4. "Is this website trying to sell me something?"
  5. "Does this article include the counter-argument?"

Share with a partner. Which criterion is the hardest to check quickly?

📖 2. Cross-Referencing — The Three-Source Rule

Even the most careful analysis of a single source can be wrong. The most reliable way to check information is to cross-reference — compare the same information across at least three independent sources.

Follow this rule:

  • Check three different sources.
  • If they all agree → the information is more likely reliable.
  • If they disagree → investigate further. Something may be wrong, or the sources may be looking at different data.
Example: You read that a new law has passed. Source 1 is a viral social media post. Source 2 is a news article from a major outlet. Source 3 is the official government website. Only when all three agree can you be confident the law is real.

Activity 2 — Cross-Reference Practice 10 min

Your teacher will give you a topic (e.g., "Does vitamin C prevent colds?"). Find or imagine three sources — one from a government or university, one from a news site, one from a social media post.

For each source, write:

  1. Authority — who wrote it?
  2. Accuracy — can you verify it?
  3. Objectivity — is it biased?
  4. Currency — is it current?
  5. Coverage — does it cite sources?

Which source is the most reliable? Which is the least? Why?

📖 3. Signs of an Unreliable Source

Some sources are easy to spot once you know the warning signs. Watch for these red flags:

  • No author or organization named.
  • Emotional or sensational language — "SHOCKING!", "You won't believe..."
  • No sources cited — claims without evidence.
  • Broken links — the source may be fake.
  • Ads everywhere — the site is designed for clicks, not information.
  • Requests for money or personal data — a common scam sign.
  • Everything is one-sided — no counter-arguments or context.
  • URL looks suspicious — extra letters, odd domains, or impersonations.
Rule of thumb: If you feel emotionally pushed to react before you have time to think, be more cautious, not less.

Activity 3 — Spot the Red Flags 10 min

Each description below contains at least one red flag. Identify the flag(s) and explain what you would do.

  1. A website claims: "SHOCKING discovery! Doctors hate this one trick!" No author is listed.
  2. An article cites "a recent study" but has no link or journal name.
  3. A social media post asks you to share your full name and phone number to receive a prize.
  4. A "news" site has 45 ads per page and no "About" section.
  5. A blog post says "everyone knows" something is true but gives no evidence.

Share your findings with the class. Which red flag is the hardest to spot?

🔍 Case Study — Two Websites, Two Claims

A Grade 9 student searched "Is coffee bad for you?" and found two websites with opposite claims.

Website A: "COFFEE DESTROYS YOUR BODY — Here's the TRUTH they don't want you to know!" No author. Ads for "detox tea" on every page. No sources cited.

Website B: A university health article written by a licensed dietitian. It cites four peer-reviewed studies and explains both benefits and risks. Updated last year.

Student's evaluation:

  • Authority: Website A — none. Website B — licensed professional.
  • Accuracy: Website A — unverifiable. Website B — peer-reviewed studies.
  • Objectivity: Website A — selling detox tea. Website B — neutral.
  • Currency: Website A — unknown. Website B — recent.
  • Coverage: Website A — one-sided. Website B — covers both sides.

Conclusion: Website B is the reliable source. Website A is designed to sell a product, not to inform.

Reflect: Have you ever trusted a website just because it appeared first in a search? What will you do differently now?

🛠️ Hands-On Task — Evaluate a Source

Working in pairs, choose one real website (your teacher can provide a list or you can choose from a topic you care about).

  1. Open the website and read the main article.
  2. Evaluate the source using the five criteria: Authority, Accuracy, Objectivity, Currency, Coverage.
  3. For each criterion, write one sentence of evidence.
  4. Cross-reference the main claim with two other sources.
  5. Write a one-paragraph verdict: is the source reliable? Why or why not?

When finished, present your evaluation to another pair. Discuss: did you agree on the verdict? Why?

📝 Reflection & Review

  1. List the five criteria for evaluating source reliability.
  2. Why is cross-referencing important?
  3. Name three red flags of unreliable sources.
  4. What was the strongest source you found today, and why?

🔑 Key Takeaways

  • Evaluate sources with five criteria: Authority, Accuracy, Objectivity, Currency, Coverage.
  • Cross-reference with at least three independent sources.
  • Watch for red flags: no author, sensational language, no citations, ads, requests for money.
  • Information literacy is about habits, not luck.
