🧭 Lesson Opener

"Imagine two classrooms. In one, every student scored exactly 80. In the other, scores ranged from 40 to 100. Both have an average of 80. So which class is doing better?"

The average alone does not tell the whole story. To really understand data, you need more powerful tools: median, mode, range, and standard deviation. In this lesson, you will learn how to calculate these statistics and how to use them to interpret your survey results.

📖 1. Beyond the Average — The Five Key Statistics

Different statistics tell you different things about a dataset. Here are the five you need.

1) Mean (Average)

The sum of all values divided by the number of values. It gives the "central" value but is sensitive to outliers.

2) Median

The middle value when the data is sorted. If there are two middle values, average them. The median is less affected by outliers than the mean.

3) Mode

The value that appears most often. Mode is useful for categories (like favorite snack) where "average" doesn't make sense.

4) Range

The maximum value minus the minimum value. Range tells you how spread out the data is.

5) Standard Deviation

A measure of how far, on average, each value is from the mean. A small standard deviation means the data is tightly clustered; a large one means it is spread out.

Example data set: 3, 5, 5, 7, 9
Mean: (3+5+5+7+9)/5 = 29/5 = 5.8
Median: the middle value = 5
Mode: 5 (appears twice)
Range: 9 − 3 = 6
Standard deviation: about 2.1

📖 2. Using Spreadsheets to Process Data

If you have 100 responses in a survey, calculating everything by hand is slow and error-prone. Spreadsheets (like Google Sheets) do it instantly. Here are the key functions:

FunctionWhat It DoesExample
=AVERAGE(A2:A100)MeanAverage of a range
=MEDIAN(A2:A100)MedianMiddle value
=MODE(A2:A100)ModeMost frequent
=MAX(A2:A100)-MIN(A2:A100)RangeMax minus min
=STDEV(A2:A100)Standard deviationSpread of data

You can also use =COUNTIF(A2:A100, ">50") to count how many values are greater than 50.

Tip: Always sort your data before looking at the median. A spreadsheet does it automatically, but if you are working by hand, sorting helps you avoid mistakes.

📖 3. Outliers and Why They Matter

An outlier is a value that is very different from the rest of the data. For example, if most students spend 1–3 hours on their phones, but one student reports 15 hours, that 15 is an outlier.

Outliers matter because they can pull the mean far away from the "typical" value.

Example: Test scores: 70, 75, 78, 80, 82, 85, 100.
Mean = 81.4
Median = 80
The 100 is an outlier. It pulls the mean up. The median (80) is more representative of the typical score.

When you find an outlier, do not automatically delete it. Instead, ask: Why is this value so different? Is it a mistake in the data? Or is it real? Sometimes outliers reveal important information.

🔍 Case Study — Processing Class Phone Usage Data

A Grade 9 class collected survey data on daily phone usage (in hours). Here are the results from 20 students:

2, 3, 3, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5, 5, 5, 6, 6, 6, 7, 7, 8, 8, 9, 10, 15

Statistics computed in Google Sheets:

  • Mean: 6.0 hours
  • Median: 5.5 hours
  • Mode: 5 hours
  • Range: 15 − 2 = 13 hours
  • Standard deviation: 3.0

Interpretation:

  • The typical student uses their phone for about 5–6 hours per day.
  • The 15-hour value is an outlier — it pulls the mean higher than the median.
  • The wide range (13 hours) tells us that usage varies a lot across students.

Reflect: What decision might this data support? For example, should the school offer a "digital balance" workshop?
