⚖️ Lesson 4 — Distinguishing Fact from Opinion

⏱️ 100 minutes 📘 Indicator 3 🗓️ Semester 2 · Midterm 🎓 Grade 9

🧭 Lesson Opener

"Bangkok is the capital of Thailand. Bangkok is the best city in the world. One of these statements can be proven. The other cannot. Can you tell which is which?"

Facts and opinions are both useful — but they are not the same. Facts can be verified. Opinions express personal views. When you can separate them, you can read more carefully, argue more clearly, and avoid being misled. In this lesson, you will learn to spot the difference — and to identify the tricky mixed statements that combine both.

Right side: a speech bubble with the word "Opinion" and a lightbulb. Between them, a balance scale tilts toward the fact side. Warm bronze and parchment tones.

🎯 What You Will Learn

  • Define fact, opinion, and belief.
  • Identify signal words that reveal opinions.
  • Recognize mixed statements that combine fact and opinion.
  • Analyze a news article and separate its facts from its opinions.

📖 1. Fact, Opinion, and Belief

These three categories look similar but are very different.

Fact

A statement that can be proven true or false using evidence. Facts are objective. Example: "The Mekong River is about 4,350 km long."

Opinion

A personal view, judgment, or feeling. Opinions cannot be proven true or false — they belong to the person who holds them. Example: "Thai food is the best in the world."

Belief

A strong conviction, often based on values, culture, or faith. Beliefs may be held even without evidence and are not usually changed by facts. Example: "Honesty is always the right choice."

Key idea: Facts can be checked. Opinions cannot. Beliefs are deeply held and personal. A good reader always knows which one they are reading.

Activity 1 — Classify the Statement 8 min

Read each statement and write F for fact, O for opinion, or B for belief.

  1. Bangkok is the capital of Thailand.
  2. Bangkok is the best city in the world.
  3. The Mekong River is 4,350 km long.
  4. Thai food is delicious.
  5. Everyone should be kind to others.
  6. Exercise improves heart health.
  7. Sundays are the best day of the week.

Compare with a partner. Which ones were tricky?

📖 2. Signal Words for Opinions

Opinions often use specific words that reveal a personal view. Learning these signal words helps you spot opinions quickly.

Signal WordExample
"I think""I think this movie is the best."
"In my opinion""In my opinion, this is the wrong choice."
"Best / worst""This is the worst test ever."
"Should""Students should not have homework."
"Beautiful / boring""The building is beautiful."
"Believe""I believe this is unfair."
Tip: Facts often include numbers, dates, names, and verifiable events. When you see numbers, ask: "Can this be checked?" If yes, it's likely a fact.

Activity 2 — Spot the Signal Words 10 min

Highlight or underline the signal word(s) in each statement, then classify it as fact or opinion.

  1. "This is the best phone on the market."
  2. "Thailand has 77 provinces."
  3. "I think the new policy is unfair."
  4. "The 2024 election had a 75% turnout."
  5. "Running is more fun than swimming."
  6. "Everyone should exercise every day."
  7. "The Eiffel Tower is in Paris."
  8. "The Eiffel Tower is the most beautiful landmark."

📖 3. Mixed Statements — The Trickiest Type

Some statements mix facts and opinions in a single sentence. These are the hardest to classify. You need to separate the sentence into its factual part and its opinion part.

Example: "The movie was 2 hours long and boring."
Fact: The movie was 2 hours long.
Opinion: The movie was boring.

Mixed statements are common in news, ads, and political speech — because they can slip an opinion into a factual-sounding sentence.

Image 4.4 — Mixed Statement Description: A parchment card showing a sentence with two colors: the factual part in blue, the opinion part in red. Below the sentence, two boxes labeled "Fact" and "Opinion." Warm bronze tones.

Activity 3 — Split the Mixed Statements 10 min

For each mixed statement, write the fact part and the opinion part separately.

  1. "The Eiffel Tower is 330 m tall and worth visiting."
  2. "The new policy increased taxes and hurt the economy."
  3. "Our school has 800 students and the best teachers in the district."
  4. "The coffee contains caffeine and tastes terrible."
  5. "The team won the championship and played beautifully."

Compare with a partner. Did you split the sentences the same way?

🔍 Case Study — Analyzing a News Article

Read the short news article below. Notice how facts and opinions are mixed together.

"The city council voted 7–2 on Tuesday to approve the new park. The park will cover 5 hectares and cost 30 million baht. Supporters say it will be a wonderful addition to the community and improve the quality of life for residents. Critics argue it is a wasteful use of public money and should have been spent on roads."

Facts:

  • The city council voted 7–2 on Tuesday.
  • The park will cover 5 hectares.
  • The cost is 30 million baht.

Opinions:

  • "A wonderful addition to the community" (supporters' opinion).
  • "Improve the quality of life" (supporters' opinion, unless backed by data).
  • "Wasteful use of public money" (critics' opinion).
  • "Should have been spent on roads" (critics' opinion).

Reflect: Notice that both sides of the story are represented. That is a sign of fair reporting. But even here, some opinions sound like facts — especially when no data is provided to support them.

🛠️ Hands-On Task — Analyze an Article

Working in pairs, choose a short news article or opinion column. Then:

  1. Highlight or underline all facts in one color.
  2. Highlight all opinions in another color.
  3. Highlight mixed statements in a third color.
  4. Write a summary of the article using only the facts (no opinions).

When finished, compare your summary with another pair. Did you use the same facts? Did your summaries agree?

📝 Reflection & Review

  1. What is the difference between a fact and an opinion?
  2. Name three signal words that usually indicate an opinion.
  3. What is a mixed statement, and why are they tricky?
  4. Why is it important to separate fact from opinion when reading news?

🔑 Key Takeaways

  • Facts can be verified; opinions cannot.
  • Beliefs are strong convictions, often based on values.
  • Signal words like "I think," "best," and "should" indicate opinions.
  • Mixed statements combine facts and opinions — split them apart to analyze clearly.
