🏆 Lesson 10 — Final Project: Media Analysis Report

⏱️ 100 minutes 📘 Indicator 3 🗓️ Semester 2 · Midterm 🎓 Grade 9

🧭 Lesson Opener

"You now know how to evaluate sources, apply PROMPT, spot fallacies, trace origins, and analyze media. Now it's time to put it all together — one complete media analysis."

In this final project for Indicator 3, you will analyze one media message from start to finish. You will evaluate its reliability, apply the PROMPT method, identify fallacies, analyze impact, and propose a response strategy. Your final product will be a written report and a short presentation.

Image 10.1 — Lesson Opener Description: A warm parchment banner showing a large golden laurel wreath around a media message (a news article with a magnifying glass). Around it, small tools: a magnifying glass, a scroll, a compass, and a scale. Warm bronze tones.

🎯 What You Will Learn

  • Apply the full media analysis process to a real message.
  • Combine source evaluation, PROMPT, fallacy identification, and impact analysis.
  • Propose a response strategy to misinformation.
  • Present a media analysis report with evidence.

📖 1. Your Project Task

Your task is to choose one media message (a viral social media post, a news article, an advertisement, a political message, a health claim, or a product review) and write a full media analysis report.

Your report must include:

  1. A clear topic and description of the media message.
  2. Source reliability evaluated using the 5 criteria: authority, accuracy, objectivity, currency, coverage.
  3. PROMPT analysis: Primary source, Reasoning, Origin, Motive, Proof, Timeliness.
  4. At least 2 logical fallacies identified (if present).
  5. Impact analysis: individual, social, economic, political, or health.
  6. A response strategy using the seven strategies and the truth sandwich.
  7. A 5-minute presentation to the class.
  8. A short written reflection.
Tip: Choose a message you have actually seen. Real examples are always stronger than invented ones.
Image 10.2 — Project Options Description: A parchment grid with six panels showing different media messages: a viral social media post, a news article, an advertisement, a political message, a health claim, a product review. Each panel has a small icon and label. Warm bronze frames.

Activity 1 — Choose Your Media Message 10 min

In your notebook, write:

  1. The media message: What are you analyzing? (Include a link, screenshot, or full text.)
  2. Why you chose it: What interests you about this message?
  3. What you already suspect: Is it reliable, misleading, or false? Why?

Get your choice approved by your teacher before moving on.

📖 2. Project Planning

Before you write, plan your analysis using the sections below.

Step 1 — Source Evaluation

For each of the five criteria, write one sentence with evidence:

  • Authority: Who wrote it?
  • Accuracy: Can you verify the facts?
  • Objectivity: Is there bias?
  • Currency: When was it published?
  • Coverage: Does it cover the topic fully?

Step 2 — PROMPT Analysis

Apply each PROMPT step and write one sentence per step:

  • P — Primary Source
  • R — Reasoning
  • O — Origin
  • M — Motive
  • P — Proof
  • T — Timeliness

Step 3 — Fallacies

Identify any logical fallacies used in the message. Name at least two if possible, with an example for each.

Step 4 — Impact

What impact could this message have? (Individual, social, economic, political, or health.)

Step 5 — Response Strategy

How would you respond to this message? Write a truth sandwich response using the seven strategies.

Image 10.3 — Project Plan Template Description: A parchment template with five labeled sections: Source Evaluation, PROMPT, Fallacies, Impact, Response Strategy. Each has blank lines for notes. A quill and ink pot at the bottom right. Warm parchment tones.

Activity 2 — Write Your Plan 15 min

In your notebook, complete the five planning steps. Be specific and use evidence from the media message.

Show your plan to your teacher before moving to the write-up phase.

📖 3. Writing Your Report

Now it is time to write your full media analysis report. Follow your plan.

Recommended workflow:

  1. Write an introduction: describe the media message and why you chose it.
  2. Write the Source Evaluation section (5 criteria with evidence).
  3. Write the PROMPT section (6 steps).
  4. Write the Fallacies section (with examples).
  5. Write the Impact section (who is affected and how).
  6. Write the Response Strategy (truth sandwich + seven strategies).
  7. Write a conclusion: your final verdict on the message.
  8. Prepare your 5-minute presentation.

Keep your plan on your desk. Refer to it often. If you get stuck, ask your teacher or a classmate for help.

Time check: You have about 25 minutes to work on your report in class. Focus on getting the analysis clear before polishing the presentation.
Image 10.4 — Writing Your Report Description: A cartoon student at a desk writing a report on parchment. On the desk: a laptop showing the media message, a notebook with notes, and a small stack of reference scrolls. Warm bronze tones.

Activity 3 — Check Your Work 10 min

Before presenting, check your report:

  1. Did you evaluate all 5 source criteria?
  2. Did you apply all 6 PROMPT steps?
  3. Did you name and give examples of at least 2 fallacies?
  4. Did you describe the impact using specific evidence?
  5. Did your response strategy use the truth sandwich?
  6. Is your final verdict clear and supported?

Fix anything that is missing before presenting.

🔍 Case Study — A Strong Media Analysis

Last year, a Grade 9 student named Nara analyzed a viral TikTok video claiming a new "miracle fruit" could cure diabetes.

Source evaluation: No author name, no medical credentials, no citations. The video had over 1 million views but no evidence.

PROMPT analysis: No primary source (no study existed), reasoning was flawed (one person's claim is not proof), origin was an anonymous account, motive was likely financial (selling a product), proof was nonexistent, and the video was recent but recycled similar false claims from years before.

Fallacies: Appeal to emotion ("imagine living without diabetes"), hasty generalization ("my uncle tried it and felt better"), and appeal to authority ("a traditional healer says...").

Impact: Health — people might stop taking real medicine and rely on the fruit instead.

Response strategy: A respectful truth sandwich with a link to a diabetes organization.

Result: Nara's report received top marks — not because she found a shocking answer, but because her analysis was thorough and evidence-based.

Reflect: What made Nara's report strong? (Hint: she analyzed each aspect systematically and used real evidence.)

🛠️ Hands-On Task — Prepare Your Presentation

You have about 15 minutes to prepare a 5-minute presentation. Your presentation must include:

  1. The media message — show it to the class.
  2. Source evaluation — the 5 criteria in brief.
  3. PROMPT analysis — the 6 steps in brief.
  4. Fallacies identified — at least 2.
  5. Impact — who is affected and how.
  6. Response strategy — your truth sandwich.
  7. Your verdict — is the message reliable, misleading, or false?

Practice with a partner. Give each other feedback on clarity and time.

Image 10.6 — Presentation Setup Description: A cartoon student practicing a presentation in front of a partner. A tablet shows a slide with the media message, analysis sections, and the verdict. Warm parchment tones.

📖 4. Presenting Your Project

Each student presents for 5 minutes. While you are not presenting, take notes:

  • What was the media message?
  • Was the analysis convincing?
  • What was the strongest part?
  • What would you have done differently?

After all presentations, the class will discuss: which reports were most convincing, and why?

Image 10.7 — Class Presentations Description: A cartoon classroom with a student presenting at the front. A large screen shows a media message and analysis sections. The audience listens and takes notes. The teacher smiles and nods. Warm parchment tones.

📝 Presentation & Reflection

After all presentations, write a short reflection in your notebook:

  1. What was the hardest part of your project?
  2. How has this unit changed the way you read information?
  3. What would you improve if you had one more week?
  4. What did you learn from watching other students' presentations?

🔑 Key Takeaways

  • A media analysis report combines source evaluation, PROMPT, fallacies, impact, and response.
  • Every claim should be supported with specific evidence.
  • A good response strategy uses the truth sandwich and respects the reader.
  • Media literacy is a lifelong habit, not just a school lesson.
