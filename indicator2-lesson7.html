📈 Lesson 7 — Data Visualization and Storytelling

⏱️ 100 minutes 📘 Indicator 2 🗓️ Semester 1 · Final 🎓 Grade 9

🧭 Lesson Opener

"Imagine a page full of numbers. Now imagine a single chart that tells you exactly what those numbers mean — at a glance. Which one would you rather look at?"

Numbers alone are hard to understand. Charts turn numbers into pictures. In this lesson, you will learn how to choose the right chart for the right data, how to build clear visuals, and how to tell a data story that convinces your reader.

Image 7.1 — Lesson Opener Description: A warm parchment banner split in two. Left side: a dense page of numbers with a confused face. Right side: a colorful bar chart with a smiling face. Above them, a caption reads "Which would you rather read?" Warm bronze and parchment tones.

🎯 What You Will Learn

  • Choose the right chart type for different data.
  • Create clear, effective visualizations.
  • Tell a data story with a beginning, middle, and end.
  • Highlight the key insight so readers see it instantly.

📖 1. Choosing the Right Chart

Not every chart works for every data set. Here is a simple guide.

Chart Type Best For Example
Bar chart Comparing categories Favorite snacks across students
Line graph Showing change over time Temperature over a week
Pie chart Showing parts of a whole Percentage of students by grade level
Scatter plot Showing relationships Study hours vs. test scores
Histogram Showing distribution Phone usage hours across students
Key rule: Ask "What question am I answering?" — then pick the chart that answers that question most clearly.

Activity 1 — Choose the Right Chart 8 min

For each question, write which chart type you would use and why.

  1. How many students chose each of five different snacks?
  2. How has the school's population changed over the last 10 years?
  3. What percentage of the school are Grade 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, and 12?
  4. Is there a relationship between study hours and test scores?
  5. How are phone usage hours distributed across students?

Compare with a partner. Did you both choose the same chart?

📖 2. Building a Clear Visualization

Even with the right chart type, a poor visualization can confuse readers. Follow these rules:

  1. Clear title. The title should say what the chart shows, not just "Chart 1."
  2. Labeled axes. Every axis needs a title and units (hours, THB, %).
  3. Readable font. If you can't read it from the back of the room, make it bigger.
  4. Not too many colors. 3–5 colors is usually enough.
  5. Highlight the key point. Use a different color or a callout for the most important value.
  6. Add a source note. Say where the data came from and when.
Bad title: "Data"
Good title: "Daily Phone Usage Among Grade 9 Students (2024 Survey, n=45)"

📖 3. Telling a Data Story

A data story combines numbers, charts, and words to send a clear message. Every good data story has four parts:

  1. The question: What are you trying to find out?
  2. The data: What did you collect, and how?
  3. The insight: What did you find? (This is the heart of the story.)
  4. The recommendation: What should happen next?

A 4-slide presentation works perfectly:

  • Slide 1: The question.
  • Slide 2: The data (collection method + chart of the raw data).
  • Slide 3: The insight (comparison or analysis chart).
  • Slide 4: The recommendation (with evidence).
Tip: A good data story shows one clear insight. Do not try to show everything. Pick the most important finding and build the story around it.

Activity 3 — Sketch Your Data Story 10 min

Using the survey data your group collected in Lesson 2, sketch a 4-slide data story in your notebook.

  1. Slide 1: Write your question.
  2. Slide 2: Describe the data (how you collected it, how many responses).
  3. Slide 3: What is your key insight? What chart would show it?
  4. Slide 4: What is your recommendation?

Share your sketch with your group. Which slide is strongest? Which needs work?

🔍 Case Study — A Data Story That Changed a School

A group of Grade 9 students in Chiang Mai collected survey data on school lunch preferences. Here is how they told their data story.

Slide 1 — The Question:
"What should our school serve for lunch to make students happier and healthier?"

Slide 2 — The Data:
A survey of 120 students (random sample, 30 from each grade). The bar chart showed the top three current lunches and their satisfaction scores (1–5).

Slide 3 — The Insight:
A comparison chart showed that when a vegetarian option was available, satisfaction rose from 2.8 to 4.1. Only 15% of students had ever tried the vegetarian option, but 70% said they would try it if it were available.

Slide 4 — The Recommendation:
"We recommend adding one vegetarian lunch option every day. Our data shows this would raise satisfaction scores significantly, especially for students who currently bring their own food."

Result: The school added a vegetarian option the following month. Satisfaction scores went up by 30%.

Reflect: Why was the third slide (the insight) the most important part of the story?

🛠️ Hands-On Task — Create Your Data Story

Working in your group, create a 4-slide data story using your own survey data.

  1. Slide 1: Write the question your data answers.
  2. Slide 2: Describe your data collection (method, sample size) and show a chart of the raw data.
  3. Slide 3: Present your key insight with a second chart (a comparison, a trend, or a distribution).
  4. Slide 4: Write a recommendation with evidence from your charts.

Create your slides in Google Slides, PowerPoint, or on paper. Each slide should have one clear message, one chart, and minimal text.

When finished, present your data story to the class. Each group has 4 minutes: 3 minutes to present and 1 minute for questions.

📝 Reflection & Review

  1. What chart would you use to show change over time? Why?
  2. What are three rules for making a clear visualization?
  3. What are the four parts of a data story?
  4. What was the strongest part of your group's data story?

🔑 Key Takeaways

  • Choose the right chart for the question you are answering.
  • Clear visualizations have a title, labeled axes, and a source note.
  • A data story has four parts: question, data, insight, recommendation.
  • The best data stories focus on one clear insight.
