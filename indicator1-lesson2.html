📖 1. Why Planning Comes First

When beginners want to build an app, they often rush straight to the code. They open a coding tool and start typing. Within an hour, they are stuck — the app is messy, buttons don't work, and they have forgotten what the app was supposed to do.

Professional developers never start with code. They start with planning. Planning saves time, prevents confusion, and keeps the project focused on the user's real problem.

Think of it this way: Building an app without a plan is like building a house without a blueprint. You might end up with walls, but you won't know where the doors should go.

To make planning easier, developers break the work into six clear steps. Each step answers a different question. Together, they form a pathway from a raw idea to a working app.

📖 2. The Six Steps of App Development

Every app — from a simple calculator to a giant social media platform — passes through the same six steps.

Step 1 — Requirements

Ask: What problem does the app solve? Who is the user? What features are needed? This step produces a short list of requirements. If you skip this, you won't know when your app is finished.

Step 2 — Design

Plan the screens, the user flow, and the interface. This step is where you sketch what the app will look like and how the user will move through it.

Step 3 — Prototype

Create a simple version — a sketch on paper, a clickable mock-up, or a rough model. The prototype lets you test the idea before writing real code.

Step 4 — Develop

Write the code or build the app using a tool like AppInventor or Python. This is the longest step, but because you have a plan, you know exactly what to build.

Step 5 — Test

Try the app with real users. Find bugs, note what confuses people, and record what works. Every app has bugs — testing is how you catch them.

Step 6 — Deploy and Maintain

Release the app (share it with friends, publish it, or install it) and keep updating it based on feedback. Apps are never truly "done" — they grow over time.

Worked example — Currency Converter:
  1. Requirements: convert THB to USD, easy to use, works offline.
  2. Design: one screen, two inputs (amount + rate), one output.
  3. Prototype: hand-sketch the screen on paper.
  4. Develop: build the blocks in AppInventor.
  5. Test: try 1000 THB at 35 THB/USD → 28.57 USD.
  6. Deploy: share the app with classmates via a QR code.

📖 3. Why Each Step Matters

Each step exists for a reason. Skipping any one of them causes problems later.

Step If you skip it…
Requirements You won't know what to build or when to stop.
Design Your app will look messy and confuse the user.
Prototype You'll waste hours coding something that doesn't work.
Develop There is no app — just an idea.
Test Bugs remain, users get frustrated, the app fails.
Deploy and Maintain No one uses your app, and it stops working over time.
Important: These steps are not always one-and-done. Professional developers often iterate — they go back to earlier steps to improve the app after testing. That is why the process is a loop, not a straight line.

🔍 Case Study — How the BMI Calculator Was Built

A school decided to run a health fair. The teachers asked a group of Grade 9 students to build a simple app that visitors could use to check their body mass index (BMI). Here is how the students walked through the six steps.

Step 1 — Requirements. The team asked: "What does the app need to do?" They decided it must (a) accept weight and height, (b) calculate BMI, (c) show a category (Underweight / Normal / Overweight), and (d) work on any phone in under 5 seconds.

Step 2 — Design. They sketched one screen with a title, two input boxes, one big Calculate button, and one output line. They chose soft blue and white for the colors, and a large font for accessibility.

Step 3 — Prototype. On paper, they drew the screen and asked three classmates to "click" the buttons. The classmates found the layout easy — so the team kept it.

Step 4 — Develop. Using AppInventor, they added two TextBoxes, one Button, and one Label. They wrote a small block that read the two values, computed BMI, and chose a category with if / else if / else.

Step 5 — Test. They tried: (60 kg, 1.65 m) → 22.0 "Normal". (45 kg, 1.65 m) → 16.5 "Underweight". (95 kg, 1.65 m) → 34.9 "Overweight". All results matched the school nurse's chart — the app worked.

Step 6 — Deploy and Maintain. They shared the app with a QR code at the health fair. Within a week, 200 students had used it. Based on feedback, they later added a "Reset" button and rounded BMI to 1 decimal place.

Reflect: Which step do you think took the longest? Why? Which step do you think was the most fun?

🛠️ Hands-On Task — Write Your 6-Step App Plan

Use the app idea you chose in Activity 3 (Lesson 1) or invent a new one. In your notebook, write a full 6-step plan:

  1. Requirements — What problem? Who is the user? What features?
  2. Design — Sketch a rough screen (one or two screens).
  3. Prototype — Describe the mock-up you would make (paper, digital, clay, etc.).
  4. Develop — Which tool would you use? (AppInventor, Python, Scratch.)
  5. Test — List three test cases with inputs and expected outputs.
  6. Deploy — How would you share it? (QR code, link, install file.)

Take 12 minutes to write. If you finish early, sketch a clean version of your design.

🔑 Key Takeaways

  • Planning before coding saves time and prevents mistakes.
  • Every app goes through six steps: Requirements → Design → Prototype → Develop → Test → Deploy & Maintain.
  • The process is a loop — after deploying, developers often go back and improve.
  • A complete plan makes it much easier to build a working app.
