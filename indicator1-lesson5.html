🌐 Lesson 5 — Introduction to the Internet of Things (IoT)

⏱️ 100 minutes 📘 Indicator 1 🗓️ Semester 1 · Midterm 🎓 Grade 9

"What if your house could talk to you? What if the lights turned on before you walked in, and the air conditioner cooled the room before you got home? What if a farm could water its own plants based on the weather?"

All of this is possible through the Internet of Things (IoT) — a network of everyday devices that collect and share data over the internet. In this lesson, you will learn what IoT is, how it works, and how to design your own smart system.

📖 1. What Is IoT?

IoT stands for Internet of Things. It means taking ordinary objects — lights, watches, doors, plant pots — and connecting them to the internet so they can send information and take action automatically.

A "smart device" is any device that:

  • Collects data from its surroundings.
  • Sends that data over a network.
  • Receives commands or shares information with other devices.
Big idea: IoT connects the physical world (things you can touch) to the digital world (data and apps). Your smartwatch is a tiny computer that talks to your phone. Your smart speaker listens and speaks back.

📖 2. The Three Parts of an IoT System

Every IoT system, no matter how simple or complex, has three essential parts.

1) Sensors

Sensors collect data from the physical world. They measure things like temperature, light, motion, humidity, or soil moisture. Examples: a thermometer, a motion detector, a light sensor on your phone, a rain sensor.

2) Connectivity

Connectivity sends the sensor data to the internet. This can happen over Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, mobile data (4G/5G), or a wired connection. Without connectivity, the device is not part of IoT — it is just a standalone tool.

3) Actuators

Actuators take action based on the data. They can turn something on, move something, or make a sound. Examples: a motor, a water pump, an LED light, a buzzer, a smart lock.

Example — Smart home thermostat:
Sensor: temperature sensor reads 32°C.
Connectivity: sends data over Wi-Fi to the AC controller.
Actuator: the AC turns on to cool the room.
User: receives a notification on their phone.

IoT is not just a theory — it already powers systems all around us.

🔍 Case Study — A School Garden That Waters Itself

A school in Chiang Mai wanted to keep its garden healthy during the holidays — but no one was there to water it. They installed a simple IoT system.

Step 1 — Sensors. A soil moisture sensor was placed in each garden bed. A rain sensor was placed on the roof. Both measured the environment every 10 minutes.

Step 2 — Connectivity. The sensors sent their readings over Wi-Fi to a small controller in the school office.

Step 3 — Processing. The controller checked: "Is soil moisture below 30% AND no rain in the last 2 hours?" If yes, it decided to water the plants.

Step 4 — Actuator. A water pump turned on for 3 minutes, then turned off. A small LED on the controller lit up to show "watering in progress."

Step 5 — User. The gardener received a daily summary on her phone: "Watered twice today. Soil moisture is now 45%."

Result: The garden stayed healthy all summer, and the school saved water by only watering when needed.

Reflect: What could go wrong with this system? What would happen if the Wi-Fi stopped working?

🛠️ Hands-On Task — Design Your Own IoT System

In groups of 3–4, choose a scenario and design a full IoT system.

Choose one:

  • Smart classroom that adjusts lights and temperature.
  • Smart parking system that shows available spots.
  • Smart trash bin that alerts when full.
  • Smart health monitor for elderly people.
  • Smart farm that monitors soil and weather.

Design the following:

  1. What sensors will you use? Why?
  2. What connectivity? (Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, mobile data.)
  3. What processing rules? (if / else statements.)
  4. What actuators? What will they do?
  5. What user interface? How does the user see the data?

Draw your system on a large sheet of paper. Label every part. When you present, explain how the three parts work together.

🔑 Key Takeaways

  • IoT connects everyday objects to the internet so they can sense, share, and act.
