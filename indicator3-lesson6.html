🧭 Lesson 6 — Finding the Source Origin

⏱️ 100 minutes 📘 Indicator 3 🗓️ Semester 2 · Midterm 🎓 Grade 9

🧭 Lesson Opener

"A viral quote claims a famous person said something shocking. Thousands of people are sharing it. But did they really say it? How would you find out?"

The most reliable way to check information is to trace it back to its origin — the first person or place that said it. In this lesson, you will learn four tools for tracing the source of any claim, image, or quote, and you will practice finding the original source of a viral post.

Image 6.1 — Lesson Opener Description: A warm parchment banner showing a viral post with a famous person's image and a speech bubble. Behind the post, a trail of small footprints leads back to a small scroll labeled "Original Source." A magnifying glass follows the trail. Warm bronze tones.

🎯 What You Will Learn

  • Trace information back to its original source.
  • Use reverse image search and quote search.
  • Identify when information has been taken out of context.
  • Practice tracing a viral post to its source.

📖 1. What Is "The Origin"?

The origin of information is the very first place it appeared. Not the tweet, not the Facebook post, not the group chat message — the original source that everyone else copied from.

When information is shared, it often gets changed along the way. A photo may be cropped, a quote may be shortened, a date may be edited. By finding the original source, you can see what was actually said or shown.

Key idea: The origin is the source of truth. Everything else is a copy — and copies can change.
Image 6.2 — The Origin Description: A parchment showing a chain of forwards: a small scroll at the left (the origin) is copied, shared, changed, and shared again, becoming longer and more distorted. A magnifying glass hovers over the original scroll. Warm bronze tones.

Activity 1 — Trace the Chain 8 min

Imagine a photo was posted by a small local news page. It was then shared by a viral account, then by a friend, then by a group chat.

Answer in your notebook:

  1. Which source is the origin?
  2. Why is the origin the most reliable?
  3. What might change between the origin and the final version?

📖 2. Four Tools for Tracing the Source

1) Reverse Image Search

Google Images and TinEye let you upload a photo and find where else it appears online. This is the fastest way to check a viral image.

2) Quote Search

Search the exact quote inside quotation marks. If it only appears on low-credibility sites, it may be fake. If it appears in reliable outlets or official statements, it is more likely real.

3) Citation Tracing

If an article cites a study or a report, find the original and check whether it says what the article claims.

4) Archive Tools

The Wayback Machine (web.archive.org) shows older versions of web pages. You can see what a page said before it was edited or deleted.

Tip: Always start with the tool that matches the type of content. Image → reverse image search. Quote → quote search. Study → citation tracing. Website → archive tools.

📖 3. Understanding Context

Sometimes the information is real but has been taken out of context. This is one of the hardest forms of misinformation to spot — because the original content is genuine.

Examples of context manipulation:

  • A photo from 2010 is presented as if taken this year.
  • A quote is shortened to change its meaning.
  • A study's conclusion is overstated in a headline.
  • A video clip is edited to leave out crucial context.
Example: A quote reads: "Sugar is not the main cause of obesity in every case." A headline says: "Expert says sugar doesn't cause obesity." — The meaning has been reversed by cutting two words.

When you find the original source, always read the full context. Do not just check the quote — read the article around it.

Image 6.4 — Context Manipulation Description: A parchment card showing a full sentence on the left and a shortened version on the right. Between them, scissors cut out two words. Below, a small "?" mark. Warm bronze tones.

Activity 3 — Spot the Manipulation 10 min

Each scenario describes a case of context manipulation. Identify what was changed.

  1. A study says "moderate coffee may have some benefits." A headline says "Coffee cures disease."
  2. A photo of a sunny day from a 2018 vacation is captioned "Winter storm hits the city today."
  3. A politician says "We must consider reducing X." A post says "Politician wants to ban X."
  4. A scientific paper is peer-reviewed. A blog says it "proves" a claim the paper actually denies.

Share your findings with the class. What was the common pattern?

🔍 Case Study — The Recycled Quote

A viral post shared a quote attributed to a famous author: "Books are useless — nobody should read them."

Tracing the source:

  1. The quote was searched with quotation marks — it appeared on hundreds of memes.
  2. But when the search was narrowed to "site:.edu" (educational sites), it appeared only in a 1995 interview.
  3. The original full quote was: "It is not that books are useless, but that reading without thinking is." The viral version had cut out the second half.

Result: The quote was real, but the meaning was reversed by cutting it. A classic case of context manipulation.

Reflect: How would you respond to someone who shared this viral version? What would you say?

🛠️ Hands-On Task — Source Tracing Investigation

Working in pairs, choose one viral post (your teacher can provide a list, or you can choose one you have seen). Investigate it using these steps:

  1. Find the original source of the image (reverse image search).
  2. Find the earliest appearance of the quote or claim (quote search).
  3. Check the date and location of the original.
  4. Check the context: was the original edited or trimmed?
  5. Write a one-paragraph report: is the viral claim accurate, misleading, or false?

Present your findings to the class. Which tool was most useful? What surprised you?

📝 Reflection & Review

  1. Why is the origin of information the most reliable source?
  2. Which tool would you use first for a viral image? Why?
  3. What is context manipulation, and why is it hard to spot?
  4. What was the most surprising thing you found in your investigation?

🔑 Key Takeaways

  • The origin is the first place information appeared — the source of truth.
  • Four tracing tools: reverse image search, quote search, citation tracing, archive tools.
  • Information can be real but taken out of context.
  • Always read the full context — not just the shared portion.
