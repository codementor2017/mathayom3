🎭 Lesson 5 — Logical Fallacies

⏱️ 100 minutes 📘 Indicator 3 🗓️ Semester 2 · Midterm 🎓 Grade 9

🧭 Lesson Opener

"You can't trust his opinion on climate change — he's not even a scientist." Sounds convincing, doesn't it? But wait — is that an argument, or is it an attack on the person?"

Arguments are everywhere — in ads, in political speeches, in conversations, in your feed. Some are strong. Some are weak because they rely on logical fallacies — tricks that seem convincing but do not actually prove anything. In this lesson, you will learn to spot eight common fallacies, name them, and explain why they weaken an argument.

🎯 What You Will Learn

  • Define logical fallacy.
  • Identify and name eight common fallacies.
  • Explain why fallacies weaken an argument.
  • Create your own corrected versions of flawed arguments.

📖 1. What Is a Logical Fallacy?

A logical fallacy is an error in reasoning that makes an argument invalid. Fallacies may sound convincing on the surface, but they do not actually prove the conclusion.

Fallacies are used for many reasons — to win arguments, to distract, or simply by accident. When you can spot them, you can respond more clearly and avoid being misled.

Key idea: A fallacy is not just a "wrong opinion." It is a structural flaw in the argument itself. Even if the conclusion happens to be true, the argument that supports it is still weak.

Activity 1 — Warm-Up Argument 8 min

Read this argument: "You can't trust his opinion on climate change because he's not a scientist."

Answer in your notebook:

  1. What is the argument trying to say?
  2. Is the argument attacking the idea or the person?
  3. Does it actually prove that the person's opinion is wrong?

Share with a partner. Have you seen arguments like this before?

📖 2. Eight Common Fallacies

Learn these eight fallacies. You will see them everywhere once you know them.

1) Ad Hominem

Attacking the person, not the argument. Example: "You can't trust her opinion — she's too young."

2) Straw Man

Misrepresenting someone's argument to make it easier to attack. Example: "So you're saying we should do nothing?" (when the other person never said that).

3) False Dilemma

Presenting only two options when more exist. Example: "Either you support this policy or you hate your country."

4) Slippery Slope

Claiming one small step leads to disaster. Example: "If we allow this, soon everything will fall apart."

5) Appeal to Emotion

Using feelings instead of evidence. Example: "Think of the children!"

6) Bandwagon

"Everyone does it, so it must be true." Example: "Everyone is buying this product, so it must be good."

7) Appeal to Authority

Citing an authority outside their expertise. Example: "A famous actor says this diet works, so it must be true."

8) Hasty Generalization

Drawing a conclusion from a small sample. Example: "My two friends got sick after the vaccine, so vaccines are dangerous."

Tip: When you spot a fallacy, ask yourself: "What would a strong argument look like here?" That reframing helps you respond with evidence, not emotion.

Activity 2 — Name the Fallacy 10 min

Read each argument. Write which fallacy it uses.

  1. "You're too young to understand politics."
  2. "So you're saying we should do nothing?"
  3. "Either you support this policy or you hate the country."
  4. "If we allow this, soon everything will fall apart."
  5. "Think of the children!"
  6. "Everyone is buying this product, so it must be good."
  7. "A famous actor says this diet works, so it must be true."
  8. "My two friends got sick after the vaccine, so vaccines are dangerous."

Compare with a partner. Which fallacy was the trickiest to name?

📖 3. How to Respond to a Fallacy

When you spot a fallacy, you do not need to attack the person. Instead, you can point out the flaw in the argument and ask for real evidence.

Three response strategies:

  1. Name the fallacy politely. "That sounds like a straw man — I didn't say that. What I actually said was..."
  2. Ask for evidence. "What data supports that claim?"
  3. Offer a stronger argument. Instead of arguing against the fallacy, present your own evidence-based reasoning.
Example response:
Fallacy: "You can't trust her opinion on climate change — she's not a scientist."
Response: "Whether she is a scientist or not doesn't change the evidence. Let's look at the actual data she is citing."

Activity 3 — Rewrite the Argument 10 min

Choose three arguments from Activity 2. For each, rewrite the argument without the fallacy. Use evidence and calm reasoning.

Example:

  • Original: "Everyone is buying this product, so it must be good."
  • Rewrite: "This product has been purchased by many people, but its quality depends on specific features. Let's compare the features with our needs."

Share your rewrites with a partner. Discuss: which fallacy was the easiest to rewrite?

🔍 Case Study — Fallacies in a Political Speech

Read this short excerpt from a fictional speech. Notice how many fallacies you can spot.

"My opponent is too inexperienced to have an opinion on this issue. If we allow his plan, soon everything will fall apart. Either you support my plan or you want our country to fail. Think of the children — do you want them to grow up in a broken country? Everyone I know supports my plan. Trust me, I've been in politics for 30 years."

Fallacies spotted:

  • "Too inexperienced" — Ad hominem.
  • "Soon everything will fall apart" — Slippery slope.
  • "Either you support my plan or you want our country to fail" — False dilemma.
  • "Think of the children" — Appeal to emotion.
  • "Everyone I know supports my plan" — Bandwagon.
  • "I've been in politics for 30 years" — Appeal to authority (partially).

Reflect: Notice that not a single fact or piece of evidence was presented. The entire speech was fallacies stacked together. What would a strong version of the same speech look like?

🛠️ Hands-On Task — Create and Correct Fallacies

Working in groups of 3–4, do the following:

  1. Pick two fallacies from the list of eight.
  2. Write a definition in your own words.
  3. Write a realistic example of the fallacy.
  4. Write a corrected version of the argument — same topic, no fallacy.
  5. Present all four parts to the class.

When finished, present your work. The class will try to guess which fallacy you are describing before you reveal it.

📝 Reflection & Review

  1. What is a logical fallacy?
  2. Name three fallacies and give an example of each.
  3. Why is it better to respond with evidence than to attack the person?
  4. Which fallacy do you see most often in everyday life?

🔑 Key Takeaways

  • A logical fallacy is a flaw in reasoning that weakens an argument.
  • Common fallacies include ad hominem, straw man, false dilemma, slippery slope, appeal to emotion, bandwagon, appeal to authority, and hasty generalization.
  • Respond to fallacies with evidence and calm reasoning, not emotion.
  • Spotting fallacies protects you from manipulation.
